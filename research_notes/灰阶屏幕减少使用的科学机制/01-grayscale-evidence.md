- **[IDENTIFIED]** Dekker, C. A., & Baumgartner, S. E. (2024). "Is life brighter when your phone is not? - **Perceived overuse** decreased, **d = 0.81** (large). **[V-SECONDARY]**

### Inferences

- The user's three-study base is really **one small quasi-experiment with an unclear N (Holte & Ferraro), one lab study with unverified allocation (Holte et al.), and one within-subjects design with no control group (Dekker & Baumgartner)**. None of the three is a randomised controlled trial with a parallel control group.
- The ~38 min (Holte) vs. ~20 min (Dekker) gap is large for the same manipulation. Plausible explanations: Holte's possible self-selection inflating the estimate, different baselines, different durations, or the Dutch vs. US sample. The Dekker estimate is the more defensible one because the design controls within-person.

### Gaps

- Exact p-values, test statistics, effect sizes, and limitations sections for both Holte papers — all primary texts paywalled (403).
- Holte & Ferraro's true N and allocation method.
- Whether Holte et al. (2023) randomised.

## Key Question 2: Are there randomized controlled trials? Preregistered studies?

### Takeaway

**No.** I found no randomised controlled trial of grayscale with a parallel control group, and no preregistered grayscale study. The closest is a 2026 **quasi-randomised** crossover feasibility trial that explicitly states it was neither randomised proper nor preregistered. This is a significant weakness the user should state plainly.

### Cited Findings

- **Hagerty, J., Saretha, S., Phung, B., Le, V., Vargas, J., Khan, F., & Wright, D. (2026). "A cross-over feasibility trial of smartphone grayscale mode in medical students." *Frontiers in Digital Health*, 8, 1816095.** Published **6 July 2026** — this is newer than anything the user has. — [Frontiers (open access)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1816095/full) **[V-PRIMARY]**
  - N = 61 enrolled, **N = 51 completed** (83.6% retention). Medical students. 2 weeks total, 1 week per condition.
  - **"Alternating sequence assignment based on enrollment order" — explicitly a quasi-randomised approach, NOT randomisation proper. Not preregistered.** **[V-PRIMARY]**
  - **Primary result: ~28.42 min/day reduction.** Group A (grayscale first) β = −28.39, SE = 11.53, t(324) = −2.46, 95% CI [−51.0, −5.8], p = 0.0143. Group B β = −28.44, SE = 9.94, t(337) = −2.86, 95% CI [−48.0, −8.9], p = 0.0045. Pooled t = 2.3236, df = 50, p = 0.0243. Control-condition means 339–355 min/day. **[V-PRIMARY]**
  - **Secondary outcomes null**: "Self-reported productivity and sleep quality did not demonstrate consistent or statistically significant differences between grayscale and control conditions." **[V-PRIMARY]**
  - **Measurement weakness**: screen time was "self-reported using their smartphone's built-in tracking feature" **without screenshot verification**; participants were told to enter 0 minutes if data were unavailable (a choice that biases means downward and adds noise). **[V-PRIMARY]**
  - Authors' own limitations, verbatim-sourced: social-desirability bias, incomplete adherence, unavailable tracking data, device-setting variation; **"short duration of the intervention limited assessment of longer-term adaptation or habituation effects"**; **"blinding was not feasible due to the visible nature of the grayscale display"**; confounds from navigation/videoconferencing/travel inflating measured screen time; qualitative responses from a limited subset; **no washout period between phases**. **[V-PRIMARY]**
  - Adherence "assessed via self-report and was generally high" but **no quantified adherence percentage and no count of participants who reverted to colour** was reported. **[V-PRIMARY]**

- Dekker & Baumgartner's grayscale study is **within-subjects with no control group**, so also not an RCT. The same research group *did* run a preregistered RCT — but on notifications, not grayscale (see Q4). **[V-PRIMARY for the notification study]**

### Inferences

- Blinding is structurally impossible for this intervention (the authors of the 2026 trial say so explicitly). Every grayscale study therefore carries unavoidable **demand characteristics and expectancy effects**: participants know they are in the "reduce your phone use" condition. This is the deepest methodological problem in the literature and it cannot be fixed by a bigger N.
- Convergence is nonetheless notable: four independent samples (US college, US medical, Dutch university, plus the Holte replication) all land in the **20–40 min/day** range with the pickup-count null. That consistency is the strongest thing this literature has going for it — stronger than any individual study.

### Gaps

- No preregistered grayscale study found on PsyArXiv/OSF. My OSF and PMC search fetches failed (the OSF preprint page returned no extractable content), so I cannot rule one out — I can only say I did not find one.

## Key Question 3: Null, contradictory findings, and failed replications

### Takeaway

I found **no failed replication of the screen-time effect** and **no study reporting grayscale had zero effect on screen time**. But there is a consistent pattern of **null results on every downstream outcome that matters more than minutes**: pickups, sleep, productivity, and subjective wellbeing. There is also a direct contradiction between the two Holte papers and the others on mental health.

### Cited Findings

- **Pickup/unlock frequency null, replicated twice**: Holte & Ferraro found unlocks unchanged — [gogray.today](https://www.gogray.today/research/grayscale-mode); Dekker & Baumgartner found **d = 0.04, non-significant** — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20501579231212062). **[V-SECONDARY, two independent samples]**
- **Sleep quality null, replicated twice**: Dekker & Baumgartner (perceived sleep quality not affected) and Hagerty et al. 2026 (no significant difference). **[V-PRIMARY for Hagerty; V-SECONDARY for Dekker]**

Same lead author, opposite conclusions on affect. **[V-SECONDARY — flagging as a genuine inconsistency, not resolved]**
- **Subjective/objective dissociation**: Dekker & Baumgartner found participants did not perceive the reduction that the logs recorded. This is a contradiction *within* a single study between objective and subjective evidence. **[V-SECONDARY]**
- **Additional study found (not in the user's set), open access, could not fully extract**: "Suffering from problematic smartphone use? Why not use grayscale setting as an intervention! – An experimental study," *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* (2023), ScienceDirect PII S2451958823000271. ScienceDirect returned 403. A secondary source attributes a grayscale study in CHBR 2023 to **"Coward et al. (2023)"** reporting decreased PSU scores, reduced anxiety, and reduced screen time — and critically notes **"control participants self-selected into grayscale after learning about it,"** i.e. control contamination. — [ScienceDirect listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958823000271); [gogray.today](https://www.gogray.today/research/grayscale-mode). **[UNVERIFIED — authorship "Coward et al." is from a single low-quality secondary source and should be confirmed before citing.]**
- **Also surfaced, unverified**: a grayscale-adjacent study in pharmacy students — cited by Hagerty et al. (2026) as **Myers et al. A secondary source instead calls this "Shuler & Strasser (2023)" in the same journal. **The two sources disagree on authorship and year; I could not resolve this.** Reported findings: participants reported reduced phone appeal, increased productivity, improved sleep — all self-reported. — [Frontiers reference list](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1816095/full); [gogray.today](https://www.gogray.today/research/grayscale-mode) **[UNVERIFIED]**

- The absence of a failed replication is **weak evidence**, not strong: with only ~5 small studies, mostly by two research groups, and no preregistration, the literature is far too thin for publication bias to have been detectable. A file-drawer of null grayscale pilots would be entirely invisible.

It constrains what can honestly be claimed for a grayscale product: it buys back time, not demonstrated wellbeing.

## Key Question 4: How large is the grayscale effect vs. other digital-wellbeing interventions?

### Takeaway

Grayscale compares **favourably** to the two best-documented alternatives, because the strongest comparator evidence is largely null. The single most rigorous study in this adjacent literature — a preregistered RCT of disabling notifications — found **no effect on screen time at all**, and found notification removal *increased* FOMO. Against that benchmark, grayscale's 20–40 min/day is one of the better-supported single-feature manipulations, which says as much about the weakness of the field as about grayscale.

### Cited Findings

**Notification disabling — preregistered RCT, NULL (the key comparator):**
- Dekker, C. A., Baumgartner, S. E., & Sumter, S. R. "Beyond the Buzz: Investigating the Effects of a Notification-Disabling Intervention on Smartphone Behavior and Digital Well-Being," *Media Psychology* (DOI 10.1080/15213269.2024.2334025); thesis Chapter 2. — [UvA full text (open)](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/318963945/Chapter_2.pdf) **[V-PRIMARY — read in full]**
- Verbatim from the abstract: **"Based on a preregistered randomized controlled trial (N = 205), including objectively logged smartphone behavior data and daily mobile diary assessments of subjective experiences, we found that a one-week notification-disabling intervention did not affect smartphone behavior (i.e., checking frequency and screen time)."** **[V-PRIMARY]**
- Also from the abstract: **"we found no effects on perceived control, overuse, smartphone vigilance, productivity, and smartphone-related distraction."** The intervention *did* reduce perceived checking habit strength, and **"the absence of notifications also led to increased fear of missing out, suggesting that disabling notifications results in drawbacks rather than improvements in digital well-being."** **[V-PRIMARY]**
- Authors' framing: **"these findings challenge the assumption that notifications play a prominent role in driving smartphone use."** **[V-PRIMARY]**

**Algorithmic de-personalisation — preregistered, worked:**
- Dekker, C. A., Baumgartner, S. E., & Sumter, S. R. (2025). "For you vs. for everyone: The effectiveness of algorithmic personalization in driving social media engagement." *Telematics and Informatics*, 101, 1–12, DOI 10.1016/j.tele.2025.102300. — [UvA full text (open)](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/318963947/Chapter_3.pdf) **[V-PRIMARY]**
- **"In this preregistered study, 88 TikTok users participated in a two-week within-subjects design: a baseline week (default highly personalized feed), followed by an experimental week (less personalized feed)... We found that both daily frequency and duration of TikTok use decreased, self-regulation increased, and participants derived less enjoyment from their use."** Note this is the **same within-subjects baseline-then-intervention design** as their grayscale study — directly comparable. Objective usage obtained via screenshots. **[V-PRIMARY]**

**Self-chosen disconnection strategies in the general population — counterproductive:**
- Dekker, C. A., Sumter, S. R., & Baumgartner, S. E. (2026). "Unraveling the dynamics of perceived smartphone overuse and disconnection strategies: Longitudinal insights." *New Media & Society*, 28(2), 856–878, DOI 10.1177/14614448241303113. Six-wave longitudinal survey, **N = 1,674**, representative of the Dutch population on age/gender/education. — [UvA full text (open)](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/318963943/Chapter_1.pdf) **[V-PRIMARY]**
- Verbatim: **"using disconnection strategies in the previous two months was related to higher perceived overuse and self-reported screen time. Together, these findings suggest that people do not always take action when unsatisfied with their smartphone use, and when they do, technology-based disconnection strategies are not effective in reducing perceived smartphone overuse."** **[V-PRIMARY]**

**Multi-strategy nudge bundles — worked, but confounded for attribution:**
- A nudge-based RCT (*International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction*, DOI 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w) tested a bundle of **ten** strategies that *included* switching the display to greyscale. Study 1: reductions in PSU, screen time, and depressive symptoms after 2 weeks. Study 2: reduced PSU, lower screen time, improved sleep quality vs. control; **"the brief intervention returned problematic smartphone use scores to normal levels for at least 6 weeks."** — [SpringerLink](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w) **[V-SECONDARY]**
- **Attribution caveat:** because grayscale was one of ten bundled components, this study **cannot** be used to estimate the grayscale effect. It is evidence for bundles, not for grayscale.
- Relatedly, a secondary source reports **Kasturiratna et al. (2025)**, *Digital Health*, finding that **"stacking micro-friction techniques produced larger effects than single interventions alone."** — [gogray.today](https://www.gogray.today/research/grayscale-mode) **[UNVERIFIED — single low-quality source, N and effect sizes not obtained]**

**App blockers / use-reduction apps:**
- "Evaluating the Effectiveness of Apps Designed to Reduce Mobile Phone Use and Prevent Maladaptive Mobile Phone Use: Multimethod Study," *Journal of Medical Internet Research* (2023), e42541. — [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e42541/) **[identified, content not extracted]**

### Inferences

- **Ranking the single-feature manipulations by evidence quality**, best to worst: (1) algorithmic de-personalisation — preregistered, worked, but platform-specific and not user-controllable; (2) grayscale — ~20–40 min/day, replicated across 4 samples, no RCT, acceptability poor; (3) notification disabling — preregistered RCT, **null, with a wellbeing backfire**.
- Grayscale's ~20 min/day against the Dekker group's own ~339–355 min/day control baselines (Hagerty) or a typical ~4 h/day global average is roughly a **6–10% reduction**. Honest characterisation: a real but modest nudge, not a solution to problematic use.
- The N = 1,674 longitudinal finding is the most uncomfortable datum for any grayscale product: when people adopt disconnection strategies *of their own accord in the wild*, perceived overuse goes **up**, not down. Short lab-supervised interventions may not generalise to voluntary long-term adoption — which is precisely the product use case.

### Gaps

- No head-to-head trial comparing grayscale against app blockers or phone-free time within one study.
- No meta-analysis with pooled effect sizes across digital-wellbeing intervention types.
- Could not extract the JMIR app-blocker effect sizes.

## Key Question 5: Duration of effect, habituation, and workaround behaviour

### Takeaway

**Essentially unstudied.** Every grayscale study found is 8 days to 2 weeks long. No study tracked habituation, no study reported post-intervention follow-up after colour was restored, and no study quantified how many users toggled colour back. The 2026 trial names this gap explicitly as its own limitation.

### Cited Findings

- Hagerty et al. (2026), verbatim limitation: **"The short duration of the intervention limited assessment of longer-term adaptation or habituation effects."** The same paper also had **no washout period** between crossover phases, and acknowledges **"residual behavioral adaptation cannot be entirely excluded."** — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1816095/full) **[V-PRIMARY]**
- Hagerty et al. (2026): adherence "assessed via self-report and was generally high," but **no quantified adherence percentage and no count of reversion to colour** reported. **[V-PRIMARY]**
- Durations across the whole literature: Holte & Ferraro 8–10 days; Holte et al. two lab visits (interval unverified); Dekker & Baumgartner 1 baseline week + 1 intervention week; Hagerty et al. 1 week per condition. **Maximum exposure anywhere in this literature: ~2 weeks.** **[V-SECONDARY/V-PRIMARY as noted above]**
- **Strongest available proxy for the acceptability/workaround problem**: roughly **half** of Dekker & Baumgartner's participants found grayscale annoying and **said they would not continue using it voluntarily**. — [gogray.today](https://www.gogray.today/research/grayscale-mode); [alignmateapp](https://www.alignmateapp.com/blog/does-grayscale-reduce-screen-time) **[V-SECONDARY]**
- **Nearest thing to durability evidence anywhere in the field** is the bundled nudge RCT: PSU scores held at normal levels **for at least 6 weeks** — but that is a ten-component bundle, not grayscale. — [SpringerLink](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w) **[V-SECONDARY]**

### Inferences

- Because grayscale works (per the authors' own mechanism account) by **removing sensory reward/salience**, it is a prime candidate for habituation — and the one design feature that would detect it (a multi-week or multi-month arm) does not exist in any published study. Any claim that grayscale "works long-term" is currently **unsupported by evidence in either direction**.
- The ~50% "would not continue" figure plus the finding that users **do not perceive** their own reduction together predict poor voluntary retention: the intervention delivers a benefit the user cannot feel at a cost the user can feel. That is an adherence problem, and it is the central product risk.

### Gaps

- No study measured toggle-back rates, partial compliance (e.g. disabling grayscale for photos/video), or use of iOS/Android shortcuts to switch colour on demand.
- No follow-up measurement after the intervention ended in any study, so rebound is entirely unknown.

## Key Question 6: Grayscale on desktop / browser rather than phone

### Takeaway

**I found no empirical study of grayscale on desktop or in a browser.** This appears to be a genuine evidence vacuum, which matters directly given the project context (a Chrome colour-filter extension): the entire evidence base concerns smartphones, and none of it transfers without assumption.

### Cited Findings

- All five grayscale studies located are smartphone-only: college students' phones (Holte & Ferraro 2020; Holte et al. 2023), Dutch students' phones (Dekker & Baumgartner 2024), medical students' phones (Hagerty et al. 2026), pharmacy students' phones (Myers/Shuler, unverified). **[V-PRIMARY/V-SECONDARY as noted]**
- The closest adjacent desktop-ish finding is on background *images* rather than desaturation: "How Background Images Impact Online Incivility," ACM (DOI 10.1145/3555545) — surfaced in search but **not on grayscale and not extracted**. — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3555545) **[UNVERIFIED relevance]**

### Inferences

- The phone→desktop transfer is not obviously valid. The proposed mechanism (colour salience drives hedonic reward in image/video-heavy scroll feeds) is weakest exactly where desktop use is most common — text, documents, code, spreadsheets. Conversely the mechanism should transfer reasonably well to a *desktop browser used for social/video feeds*, which is the plausible scope for a Chrome extension.
- Honest claim available to the project: grayscale has modest replicated evidence **on phones** and **no direct evidence on desktop**; a desktop implementation is a mechanism-plausible extrapolation, not an evidence-backed one.

### Gaps

## Key Question 7: Main methodological criticisms

### Takeaway

Every criticism on the user's list applies, and three are confirmed in the authors' own words. The literature's single irreparable flaw is that **blinding is impossible**, so expectancy effects cannot be ruled out in any study.

### Cited Findings

- **Blinding impossible — author-confirmed**: "blinding was not feasible due to the visible nature of the grayscale display" — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1816095/full) **[V-PRIMARY]**
- **Self-report measurement error — author-confirmed**: Hagerty et al. used self-reported screen-time figures "without screenshot verification," citing "social desirability bias, incomplete adherence to study instructions, unavailable tracking data, or variation in device settings." Mitigation: Holte & Ferraro and Dekker & Baumgartner used objective/logged data, so this criticism does not apply uniformly. **[V-PRIMARY]**
- **Short duration — author-confirmed**: see Q5. **[V-PRIMARY]**
- **No randomisation**: Hagerty et al. used alternating enrollment-order assignment, explicitly "a quasi-randomized approach rather than randomization proper"; Dekker & Baumgartner had no control group; Holte et al.'s allocation method is unverified. **[V-PRIMARY / UNVERIFIED]**
- **No preregistration** of any grayscale study found; by contrast the same group's notification and TikTok studies *were* preregistered — which makes the absence for grayscale conspicuous. **[V-PRIMARY for the comparators]**
- **Control contamination**: a secondary source reports that in the CHBR 2023 grayscale study "control participants self-selected into grayscale after learning about it." — [gogray.today](https://www.gogray.today/research/grayscale-mode) **[UNVERIFIED but serious if true]**
- **Attrition**: Hagerty et al. lost 10/61 (16.4%). Attrition for the other studies not obtained. **[V-PRIMARY]**
- **Sample narrowness**: literally every sample is students — US college, US medical, Dutch university, US pharmacy. **No general-population, adolescent, or older-adult grayscale sample exists.** **[V-PRIMARY/V-SECONDARY]**
- **Small N throughout**: 51, 61, 84, 133, and (unverified) 73–220. The largest grayscale sample is ~133. For comparison, the *null* notification RCT had N = 205 and the longitudinal survey N = 1,674 — i.e. the rigorous studies in this field are larger than any grayscale study. **[V-PRIMARY]**
- **Confounded outcome measure — author-confirmed**: "external factors such as navigation requirements, videoconferencing, and travel may have inflated measured screen time independently of attentional engagement." Total screen time is a poor proxy for problematic use. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1816095/full) **[V-PRIMARY]**

### Inferences

- **Overall honest verdict for the user: this evidence base is thinner than the three-citation framing suggests.** It is ~5 small, short, unblinded, non-preregistered student studies, two of them from the same lab, with zero RCTs with parallel controls and zero desktop studies. What it supports is a narrow claim: *grayscale produces a modest, replicated reduction in objectively measured phone screen time on the order of 20–40 min/day over 1–2 weeks in student samples, driven by shorter sessions rather than fewer pickups, with no demonstrated improvement in sleep, productivity, anxiety (inconsistent), or subjective wellbeing, and with poor user acceptability.*
- The claim it does **not** support: that grayscale improves digital wellbeing, reduces problematic use durably, works beyond two weeks, works for non-students, or works on desktop.
- A defensible rhetorical position: the convergence of four independent samples on a similar magnitude, plus the replicated pickup-null (which is a specific, mechanism-consistent prediction rather than a generic "it helped"), is more persuasive than any single study — but it is convergence among weak designs.
