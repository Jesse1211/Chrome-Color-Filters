# Transient attention, darkness, and rapid cuts — verification of three report claims

Evidence tags: **[ROBUST]** = replicated, textbook-level · **[MODERATE]** = real but limited/few studies · **[CONTESTED]** = active disagreement in the literature · **[UNSUPPORTED]** = no source found making this claim · **[REFUTED]** = evidence points the other way.

---

## CLAIM 1 — "Watching a phone in the dark is harder to put down because it is the only light source in your visual field"

### Takeaway
**VERDICT: UNSUPPORTED (mostly) — must be cut or narrowed drastically.** The *physical* premise (extreme luminance ratio, rod/cone adaptation state) is real and citable **[ROBUST]**, but the *behavioural* inference — that darkness makes a phone harder to put down — has **no supporting study**. I found no research testing session duration or disengagement difficulty as a function of ambient light. The attentional-capture literature actually argues against a simple "brightest thing wins" story, because capture is contingent on the viewer's goals (see Claim 2), and a continuously-present screen is not a transient at all.

### Cited Findings

**Dark adaptation — the physical substrate [ROBUST]**
- Cones reach maximum sensitivity in roughly 5–7 minutes and near-total adaptation in about 10 minutes; rods require 30–45 minutes or more of darkness to reach ~80% dark adaptation, with full sensitivity taking over 2 hours. Most dark adaptation occurs in the first 20–30 minutes — [CooperVision, How Eyes See at Night](https://coopervision.com/blog/how-eyes-see-at-night) (secondary/clinical-education source); mechanism corroborated by [Jiang et al., 2022, J Physiol — retinal dark adaptation tracked in vivo with the ERG](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9796346/)
- Pupil diameter ranges ~1.5 mm to ~7 mm and can change in 1–2 seconds, but pupil aperture compensates for brightness changes by a factor of **less than 100×** — i.e. the pupil alone cannot absorb the luminance ratios involved; retinal/photochemical adaptation does the rest — [CooperVision](https://coopervision.com/blog/how-eyes-see-at-night)
- Smartphone displays can be driven to luminances **below the human cone threshold (~−3 to −3.5 log cd/m²)**, which is why phones can be used as rod-dark-adaptometers at all — [Preliminary Evaluation of a Mobile Device for Dark Adaptation Measurement, PMC6343678](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6343678/)
- Viewing a smartphone measurably attenuates ERG response amplitudes in the viewing eye, with recovery over several minutes in the dark — i.e. the post-phone "temporary blindness" is genuine retinal light adaptation, not a subjective impression — [Jiang et al., 2022, J Physiol](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1113/JP283105)
- Dark adaptation depends on the **spatial** luminance distribution, not just mean luminance: any spatially non-uniform background lengthens adaptation time relative to a uniform dark background — [The effect of spatial luminance distribution on dark adaptation, Journal of Vision](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2528618)
- Disability glare works by scattering light inside the eye, laying a **homogeneous veiling luminance** across the retina (so its effect is a global contrast reduction, not a local attention magnet) — [Winter, Fotios & Völker, 2018, Lighting Research & Technology](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1477153516672719)

**Attention and surround luminance [MODERATE, and not the claim being made]**
- The size of the attentional window can be measured via attentional modulation of the pupillary light response, and window size is causally important for capture: *attention can only be captured by stimuli appearing within the attentional window* — [Nature Scientific Reports, 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-30343-7)
- Attentional selectivity narrows spatially around a target in unsegmented (low-segmentation) configurations compared with segmented ones — [The Selectivity of Task-Dependent Attention Varies with Surrounding Context, J Neurosci 32(35):12180](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/32/35/12180)

**Bedtime phone use [MODERATE for prevalence/sleep; UNSUPPORTED for the darkness mechanism]**
- 90.6% of adolescents in one sample used smartphones in bed, mean 2.3 h/day of in-bed use — [PMC9368626](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9368626/)
- 38% of US adults say pre-bed news scrolling makes their sleep slightly or significantly worse; 46% among 18–24s (self-report survey) — [American Academy of Sleep Medicine](https://aasm.org/americans-are-doomscrolling-at-bedtime-prioritizing-screen-time-over-sleep/)
- Phone use after lights-out was associated with worse sleep quality, more insomnia symptoms and more daytime fatigue in a survey of 844 adults — [PMC9643910](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9643910/)
- Problematic smartphone use predicts bedtime procrastination, with the proximate mechanism described as *difficulty stopping* — but this is attributed to problematic-use traits, **not to the dark environment** — [PMC11059092](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11059092/)
- Sources that mention darkness do so only as a **sleep/circadian** moderator ("people tend to use smartphones in the dark when lying in bed, so use can be particularly harmful to sleep quality") — never as a driver of usage duration — [PMC9643910](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9643910/)

### Inferences
- The defensible residue of Claim 1 is **purely photometric**: in a dark bedroom the phone is effectively the only photopic stimulus in the field, your retina is part-way into rod-dominated adaptation, the display can span luminances from below cone threshold to hundreds of cd/m², and the pupil cannot compensate for a ratio that large. That is all true and citable. It says nothing about wanting to stop.
- The claim as written commits two errors: (1) it treats *salience* as *stickiness* — there is no evidence that being the brightest thing in the field impairs voluntary disengagement; (2) a steady display is not a transient, and the capture literature (Claim 2) shows even genuine transients only capture when they match goal-relevant features.
- Note also an opposite-direction effect the report should be aware of: narrowing of the attentional window in a dark surround would, if anything, **reduce** capture by peripheral stimuli (the clock, a partner, the room) rather than increase the phone's grip. That is a plausible restatement but it is an inference, not a finding — do not present it as evidence.

### Gaps
- **No study found** that manipulates ambient illumination and measures phone session length, stopping latency, or self-reported difficulty disengaging. This is the single load-bearing experiment the claim would need and it does not appear to exist.
- No study found on "attentional disengagement" / "sticky attention" applied to screens in darkness. The disengagement literature I could reach is about cued-target paradigms and clinical attention bias, not ambient light.
- No evidence found for "tunnel vision under high local display contrast" as an attentional (rather than optical/glare) phenomenon.

### Recommended rewrite for the report
Drop the causal claim. Replace with something like: *"In a dark room the phone is the only bright thing in your visual field — the luminance ratio between screen and surround can exceed what the pupil can compensate for, and your retina is part-way into dark adaptation. (What this does to how long you keep scrolling has not, as far as I can find, actually been measured.)"* The parenthetical is not a weakness; it is the only honest version.

## CLAIM 2 — "The visual system treats sudden change as something that must be processed immediately — a leftover instinct from avoiding predators"

### Takeaway
**VERDICT: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED — first half right, second half invented.** Abrupt onsets and motion transients do reliably capture attention **[ROBUST]**, but capture is *contingent on the viewer's top-down attentional set*, not irresistible **[ROBUST/CONTESTED in detail]**. The "predator avoidance" evolutionary attribution is **[UNSUPPORTED]** — I found no peer-reviewed source attributing transient capture to predator avoidance. It is a popular gloss. Cut it.

### Cited Findings

**Transient / abrupt-onset capture is real [ROBUST]**
- Jonides & Yantis (1988) found abrupt-onset singletons capture attention in visual search when onset is orthogonal to the target's defining attributes, concluding abrupt onset may be *unique* among features in capturing attention — [Uniqueness of abrupt visual onset in capturing attention, Perception & Psychophysics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03208805)
- The "new object" hypothesis: Yantis & Hillstrom (1994) removed the luminance change accompanying an object's appearance and the new object *still* captured attention — evidence that novelty per se, not only the luminance transient, matters — summarised in [Attention capture by abrupt onsets: re-visiting the priority tag model, PMC3868916](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3868916/)
- Counter-current within the same debate: later work argues it is not the new object itself but the **transient signals** (motion, looming, brightness change) that typically accompany onsets that draw attention — [Do sudden onsets need to be perceived as new objects to capture attention?](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-016-1183-7); see also [Attentional Capture by Abrupt Onsets: New Perceptual Objects or Visual Masking?, JEP:HPP 22(6):1505](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0096-1523.22.6.1505) **[CONTESTED]**
- Motion contrast is "one of the most reliable visual features to capture attention in classic cuing or search paradigms" and predicts gaze location during dynamic-scene free viewing — [Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu, Media Psychology (preprint), p.5](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf) (citing Abrams & Christ 2003; Wolfe & Horowitz 2004; Carmi & Itti 2006; Mital et al. 2011)

**But capture is contingent on goals — this breaks the "irresistible instinct" framing [ROBUST]**
- Folk, Remington & Johnston (1992): involuntary shifts of spatial attention, *even those elicited by abrupt visual onsets*, are contingent on variable internal control settings. Invalid abrupt-onset precues cost performance for onset-defined targets **but not** for colour-defined targets, and vice versa — [Folk & Remington, Involuntary covert orienting is contingent on attentional control settings](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Involuntary-covert-orienting-is-contingent-on-Folk-Remington/ab2b5f2c0539bd11393f98c18aeb1c303fe2408e); [Stimulus-driven attentional capture and attentional control settings, PubMed 8331320](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8331320/)
- On the contingent-capture view, salient but goal-mismatched stimuli **do not** produce a shift of attention — explicitly contrasted with stimulus-driven accounts holding capture to be automatic and goal-independent — [Lien, Ruthruff et al., Contingent Attentional Capture by Top-Down Control Settings (ERP converging evidence)](http://www.unm.edu/~ruthruff/Lien2008capture.pdf)
- The debate is genuinely live: an alternative reading is "delayed allocation of attention" rather than true contingency — [Contingent attentional capture or delayed allocation of attention?, Perception & Psychophysics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03194470) **[CONTESTED]**
- Capture also requires the stimulus to fall inside the current attentional window — [Nature Sci Rep 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-30343-7)

**The orienting response is the right construct — and it habituates [ROBUST]**
- Sokolov (1950s) systematically studied the orienting response and documented **habituation**: repetition of a novel innocuous stimulus produces response decrement, with OR amplitude dropping almost to zero over "a dozen or so repetitions" — [Habituation of the orienting reflex and the development of Preliminary Process Theory, Neurobiology of Learning and Memory](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1074742708001226)
- Sokolov's stimulus-model comparator theory: an OR is triggered by mismatch between the stored "neuronal model" and current input ("impulses of discrepancy") — [ibid.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1074742708001226)
- Key asymmetry for the report: *the orienting reflex is exquisitely sensitive to repetition and habituates rapidly to unreinforced sensory inputs, while the **defensive** reflex is extraordinarily resistant to habituation* — [Orienting Reflex overview, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/orienting-reflex)

**The predator story [UNSUPPORTED]**
- I searched the transient-capture and orienting-response literature and found **no** peer-reviewed source attributing abrupt-onset capture to predator avoidance. The functional account actually given in these papers is about *object individuation and spatiotemporal correspondence* (establishing whether an item is new), not threat — [PMC3868916](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3868916/)
- The nearest real evolutionary claim is the **animate monitoring hypothesis** (New, Cosmides & Tooby), which concerns prioritised detection of *animals and humans* as categories. It is adjacent but a different claim: it is about *what* content is prioritised, not about *transients* or *cuts*. Using it to support Claim 2 would be a misattribution. I did not locate a full-text source for it in this round — treat as **[GAP]**, and in any case do not bolt it onto the transient claim.

**Luminance vs colour transients — the limitation the report must state [MODERATE]**
- The entire onset/transient literature is framed around **luminance** transients, motion, looming and brightness change — colour is treated as a *separate* feature dimension, and the Folk et al. design works precisely by crossing onset-defined against colour-defined targets as distinct dimensions — [PubMed 8331320](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8331320/); [Lien et al.](http://www.unm.edu/~ruthruff/Lien2008capture.pdf)
- In film specifically, what creates and breaks edit blindness is **motion** and **audio**, with no role for colour reported — [Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu, Media Psychology preprint](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)

### Inferences
- **This is the most important limitation for the whole report.** If transient capture runs on luminance, motion and onset — and colour is a separate dimension that only captures when it matches the viewer's control setting — then **greyscale should not reduce transient/orienting capture at all**. Desaturation removes chroma; it leaves every luminance transient, every motion onset and every cut fully intact. The report must state this explicitly, or it is implicitly over-claiming for its own feature.
- Corollary: a greyscale filter's plausible mechanism is about *reward/appetitive salience* of colour, not about transient attention. Those are different literatures and the report should not let one borrow the other's evidence.

### Recommended rewrite
*"Abrupt onsets and motion transients reliably recruit attention and trigger an orienting response — this is one of the better-established findings in attention research. But it is not irresistible: capture is contingent on what you are currently looking for (Folk, Remington & Johnston 1992), and the orienting response habituates within roughly a dozen repetitions (Sokolov)."* Delete the predator sentence entirely — no source makes that claim.

## CLAIM 3 — "The brain has no switch for ignoring cuts / every cut re-triggers the instinct"

### Takeaway
**VERDICT: REFUTED.** There is direct, purpose-built research showing viewers routinely *fail to notice cuts at all*, even when explicitly instructed to press a button on every cut. Match-action cuts in real Hollywood films were missed **32.4%** of the time; shot-reverse-shot cuts **25.1%**. And the orienting response habituates by design. Both halves of the claim are wrong in the literal direction stated.

### Cited Findings

**Edit blindness — the direct refutation [ROBUST]**
- Smith & Henderson (2008) coined **edit blindness**: existing match-action cuts in feature films were missed significantly more often during an explicit cut-detection task (**32.4%**) than cuts between unrelated scenes (**9.4%**) — reported in [Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu, Media Psychology preprint, p.5](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Shot-reverse-shot cuts — the most common cut type in narrative film — produced a **25.1% miss rate** — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Failing to detect a cut constitutes **global** change blindness: the entire visual scene changes and is not noticed. The authors call match-action, if it works as editors believe, "one of the most striking demonstrations of change blindness ever recorded" — [ibid., General Discussion](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Follow-up experiment (N=32 recruited, 27 analysed; 21F/11M, M age 24.61, SD 4.41; eye-tracked; explicit spacebar-on-every-cut task, ~45 min): removing **post-cut** motion significantly reduced detection time (M=597.23 ms, SD=117.4, p=.005 vs intact) and miss probability; removing **pre-cut** motion had no significant effect (M=634.07 ms, p=.111) — contradicting the editing-manual assumption that the pre-cut motion onset is what hides the cut — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- **Audio did most of the work**: cut detection was significantly faster without audio, and the effect of cut type on miss rate *disappeared* in the silent condition — edit blindness "mostly disappear[ed] without audio." Soundtrack acts as a continuous perceptual "scaffold" across the cut — [ibid., Abstract and General Discussion](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Eye movements: viewers reoriented *less* to new content across intact match-action cuts than across motion-removed cuts. A stereotypical saccadic peak occurs 250–350 ms after a cut — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Independent extension: edit blindness is **not** related to immersion/presence in Hollywood movies — [Edit blindness is not related to immersion and presence in Hollywood movies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365423070_Edit_blindness_is_not_related_to_immersion_and_presence_in_Hollywood_movies) (abstract-level only; I did not obtain full text) **[MODERATE]**

**Continuity editing exploits perception deliberately [ROBUST]**
- Edit blindness is *created* by adhering to Hollywood continuity-editing conventions, e.g. coinciding a cut with a sudden motion onset (match-action) — [ibid., Abstract](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Match-action dates to films as early as 1901 — a century of applied perceptual engineering before the psychology existed — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf) (citing Salt 2009)
- Münsterberg (1916) first noted the mismatch between perceived continuity and spatiotemporal discontinuity, hypothesising that a cut is acceptable because it *mirrors the attentional shift a viewer would naturally make* in the real world — [Smith, Film (Cinema) Perception, SAGE Encyclopedia of Perception preprint](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf)
- Formalised as Smith's **Attentional Theory of Continuity Editing** (2006) / Attentional Theory of Cinematic Continuity (2012) — [ibid.](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf)
- **Attentional synchrony**: multiple viewers' attention synchronises on dynamic real-world video as they attend to and track people; this is what lets editors predict where viewers will be looking and cut accordingly — [ibid.](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf)
- Hasson et al. (2008) neuroimaging found high inter-viewer synchronisation in language, emotion and face-processing regions during feature-film viewing — [ibid.](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf)

**General change blindness in film [ROBUST]**
- Levin & Simons: when a continuity error was inserted at every cut (e.g. a scarf vanishing), **90% of viewers who were not warned failed to spot any error**. In a clip where the actress herself changed across a cut, **66% failed to notice** — [Smith, SAGE Encyclopedia preprint, p.6–7](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf) (primary: Levin & Simons, 2000, *Media Psychology* 2, 357–380)
- d'Ydewalle et al. (1998) found a peak in saccadic activity following cuts that **violated** the 180° rule — i.e. the visual system reacts to *badly constructed* cuts, not to cuts per se — [ibid.](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf)
- Frith & Robson (1975): children shown 180°-rule-compliant films reconstructed them more accurately than children shown rule-violating versions — [ibid.](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf)

**Habituation [ROBUST]** — see Claim 2: the OR habituates almost to zero over ~a dozen repetitions ([Neurobiology of Learning and Memory](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1074742708001226)), which directly contradicts "every cut re-triggers the instinct."

**Shot-length / cut-rate data [MODERATE — real peer-reviewed data exists for film; still none for short-form video]**
- A typical 90-minute Hollywood film contains **1,000–2,000 edits**, i.e. a shot change every **2.7–5.4 seconds** (Bordwell & Thompson 2001), yet editors assume most leave the illusion of continuous action uninterrupted (Reisz & Millar 1953, p.216) — cited in [Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu preprint, p.4](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Cutting et al. (Cornell) measured shot lengths across seven decades of Hollywood film: average shot length fell from ~8–10 s in the 1960s to ~3–4 s by 2005; shot-length distributions increasingly approach a **1/f** power spectrum, with shots becoming more correlated with their neighbours over time — [Cutting, Shot Structure and Visual Activity: The Evolution of Hollywood Film](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245641122_Shot_Structure_and_Visual_Activity_The_Evolution_of_Hollywood_Film); [On Shot Lengths and Film Acts: A Revised View, *Projections* 6(1):142–145](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Cutting/publication/236964224_On_Shot_Lengths_and_Film_Acts_A_Revised_View/links/0046351a7a7618566e000000/On-Shot-Lengths-and-Film-Acts-A-Revised-View.pdf)
- Illustrative single-film figures circulating in trade press: 11.4 s (*Citizen Kane*) → 2.66 s (*Mad Max: Fury Road*) — [DFI Rentals](https://www.dfirentals.com/news/average-shot-length-in-film-citizen-kane-to-today) **[trade source, use with caution — not peer-reviewed]**

### Inferences
- The report's claim is backwards. The defensible statement is close to its opposite: **professional editing exists precisely because the brain *can* be made to ignore cuts**, and a century of craft knowledge is devoted to doing so. Short-form video inherits those techniques.
- Note a subtlety the report can use honestly: Smith's data show the cut is hidden by *motion continuity and an unbroken soundtrack*. Short-form video typically has both (continuous music bed, motion-matched or beat-matched cuts). So the mechanism is "cuts are engineered to be unnoticeable", not "cuts keep grabbing you."

### Recommended rewrite
Cut "the brain has no switch for ignoring cuts" outright and replace with: *"The brain is in fact remarkably good at ignoring cuts — in explicit cut-detection tasks viewers miss about a third of match-action cuts in real films (Smith & Henderson 2008), and the orienting response habituates within a dozen or so repetitions. What rapid cutting does is keep arousal and resource allocation elevated below the threshold of conscious notice."*

## ADDED QUESTION — What are the documented downstream consequences of rapid-cut / high-transient-density media?

### Takeaway
**The honest bottom line requested is almost exactly right: within-session arousal and resource-allocation effects of edit rate are reasonably established [MODERATE-to-ROBUST]; claims of lasting attention damage from fast pacing are NOT supported [UNSUPPORTED], and the single best-known study behind them (SpongeBob) was reinterpreted by its own authors as a *fantasy* effect, not a *pacing* effect [REFUTED as a pacing claim].**

### 1. Immediate / within-session effects

**Edit rate increases arousal AND memory — up to a point [MODERATE]**
- Lang et al., *The Effects of Edits on Arousal, Attention, and Memory for Television Messages: When an Edit Is an Edit Can an Edit Be Too Much?*: as the rate of edits (camera changes within the same visual scene) increases, **physiological arousal, self-reported arousal, and memory all increase** — [abstract via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249025611_The_Effects_of_Edits_on_Arousal_Attention_and_Memory_for_Television_Messages_When_an_Edit_Is_an_Edit_Can_an_Edit_Be_Too_Much) (full text 403; I have the abstract-level finding only — **flag as abstract-sourced**)
- Lang, *The effects of production pacing and arousing content on the information processing of television messages*: both fast pace and arousing content elicit self-reported arousal but **different patterns of physiological arousal**; both increase allocation of processing resources — **but the combination of fast pacing and arousing content can overwhelm capacity, decreasing content recognition and recall** — [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/2914177/The_effects_of_production_pacing_and_arousing_content_on_the_information_processing_of_television_messages)
- Framework: Lang's **Limited Capacity Model of Motivated Mediated Message Processing (LC4MP)** — [Lang, 2000, *Journal of Communication*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2000.tb02833.x); [LC4MP, Wiley International Encyclopedia of Media Effects](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781118783764.wbieme0077)
- Per-cut physiological and cognitive consequences, as catalogued by Smith: slowed secondary-task reaction time (Geiger & Reeves 1993), focusing of attention (Reeves et al. 1985), increased eye movements (d'Ydewalle et al. 1998; Hochberg & Brooks 1978), **improved recognition memory** (Frith & Robson 1975; Lang 1991), faster recognition for post-cut information (Carroll & Bever 1976), **heart-rate deceleration** (Lang 1990), and increased self-reported arousal (Lang et al. 2000) — [Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu preprint, p.5](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- As degree of change at a cut increases, both viewer *awareness* of the cut and *cognitive load* increase (Lang, Kurita, Gao & Rubenking 2013) — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- **Note the direction, which matters for the report:** heart-rate *deceleration* is the classic index of an *orienting* response (resource intake), not of stress arousal. And moderate edit rates *help* memory. The naive "cuts tax you and you remember less" story is only true at the high end where pacing and arousing content combine.

**Does rapid pacing make people watch longer? [UNSUPPORTED]**
- I found **no** study linking structural pacing features (edit rate, shot length) to session duration, stopping difficulty, or voluntary disengagement. This is a real gap, and it is precisely the link the report wants. Do not assert it.

### 2. After-effects

**The SpongeBob study and its reinterpretation [CONTESTED → the pacing version is REFUTED by the original authors' own follow-up]**
- Lillard & Peterson (2011, *Pediatrics*): sixty 4-year-olds randomly assigned to 9 minutes of SpongeBob SquarePants, 9 minutes of *Caillou* (slow, realistic), or free drawing; the SpongeBob group scored lower on executive function immediately afterwards. SpongeBob was roughly **three times faster than Caillou by scene-change count** — [PubMed 21911349](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21911349/?dopt=Abstract); [UVa chapter, Television and Children's Executive Function](https://uva.theopenscholar.com/files/early-development-lab/files/television_and_childrens_executive_function_8.pdf)
- Note N=60 with three arms = ~20 per cell. Small, single-site, immediate-post only. Contemporaneous criticism was substantial — [CounterPunch, The Mugging of SpongeBob](https://www.counterpunch.org/2011/09/16/the-mugging-of-spongebob/) (polemic, not evidence — cited only to document that the study was contested publicly)
- **The decisive follow-up:** Lillard et al. (2015, *Developmental Psychology*) ran three studies (Study 1 N=160, 4- and 6-year-olds; Study 2 N=60 4-year-olds). EF was impaired after *fast and fantastical* shows relative to a slow realistic show or play, and depleted equally by fast-fantastical *educational* and *entertainment* shows. If the report cites SpongeBob as pacing evidence, it is citing a result that has been superseded by its own research programme.

### 4. Resolving the tension — the crux

The apparent contradiction (cuts are often not consciously noticed, yet cuts have measurable effects) **dissolves cleanly**, and in the direction of the second option:

- Cuts produce **heart-rate deceleration** (Lang 1990) — the classic orienting-response signature — and **increase resource allocation** and arousal as edit rate rises (Lang et al.), while simultaneously being **missed ~25–32% of the time** even under explicit instruction to detect them (Smith & Henderson 2008). These are measurements at different levels: autonomic/resource-allocation vs. conscious report.
- Sokolov's framework supplies the mechanism: the OR is driven by **mismatch against a neuronal model**, and it **habituates** with repetition. A well-constructed cut minimises mismatch (motion continuity, audio scaffold) — so it does *not* produce a full conscious orienting event, while still driving the low-level resource/arousal response.
- Therefore the mechanism is **non-conscious orienting / arousal maintenance**, *not* "conscious attention capture." The report's current framing picks the wrong one.

**Single most defensible sentence:** *Raising the rate of cuts in a video reliably raises a viewer's physiological and self-reported arousal and increases the processing resources they allocate to it — and it does this largely below the threshold of conscious notice, since viewers miss roughly a quarter to a third of cuts in well-edited material even when told to watch for them.*

**Strongest caveat:** *Within-session arousal and pacing effects are reasonably well established, but nothing in this literature shows that rapid cutting makes people watch longer or harder to stop, and claims that short-form video causes lasting damage to attention are not supported — the evidence is almost entirely correlational, the one 18-day randomised intervention found no significant effect on most sustained-attention measures, and the best-known "fast pacing hurts executive function" study was reinterpreted by its own authors as an effect of fantastical content rather than pace.*

## ALSO VERIFIED (quick)

### Why the same photo looks different on different phones
**The report's "metamerism showing cracks" framing is imprecise — effectively wrong. [ROBUST]**

The correct explanation is **colour management plus display characteristics**, in roughly this order:
1. **Colour-management failure.** An image carries an embedded ICC profile telling the renderer how to interpret its pixel values; when an app ignores or discards that profile, pixel values are interpreted against the wrong primaries and colours shift — [Adobe Color Management community thread](https://community.adobe.com/t5/color-management-discussions/photos-look-different-on-my-phone-compared-to-photos-on-the-computer/m-p/13424822)
2. **Different display primaries / gamut.** OLED panels typically have a wider gamut than LCD, so the same encoded values map to more saturated extremes; black levels differ by panel technology independent of any colour management — [KTC](https://us.ktcplay.com/blogs/support-tips/why-colors-look-different-monitor-vs-phone); [Gizmodo, Why Every Smartphone Screen Looks Different](https://gizmodo.com/why-every-smartphone-screen-looks-different-1820748943)
3. **Factory calibration, brightness/white-point settings, and vendor display modes** (e.g. Samsung's Adaptive Display / AMOLED Cinema / AMOLED Photo / Basic) — two correctly colour-managed sRGB screens still differ — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/why-every-smartphone-screen-looks-different-1820748943)

**Why "metamerism breaking down" is the wrong frame:** metamerism is the phenomenon whereby *different spectral power distributions* produce the *same* colour match for a standard observer. An RGB display exploiting metamerism is what lets three primaries stand in for a continuous spectrum at all — and that mechanism is *not* failing when two phones disagree. The disagreement arises because the two displays have **different primary chromaticities and transfer functions and are being fed a differently-interpreted encoding** — a colorimetric/colour-management mismatch, not a breakdown of metameric matching. (Genuine observer metamerism failure does exist — different observers disagreeing about a metameric match, or a match holding for one observer but not another — but that is a different, much subtler phenomenon and is not what people notice when a photo looks wrong on a friend's phone.) **This is an inference from the definitions plus the cited mechanism, not a quote from a source; it is nonetheless the standard colour-science account.**

### Is transient capture luminance-driven rather than colour-driven?
**Yes, substantially — and this is a material limitation for a report about desaturation. [MODERATE]** See the "Luminance vs colour transients" block under Claim 2. The onset/transient literature is built on luminance, motion, looming and brightness change; colour is a *separate* feature dimension that captures only when it matches the viewer's current attentional control setting (that is exactly the crossed design in Folk, Remington & Johnston 1992). In the film-cut literature specifically, edit blindness is created and destroyed by **motion** and **audio**, with colour playing no reported role. **Implication the report must state: greyscale leaves every luminance transient, motion onset and cut fully intact, so it should not be expected to reduce transient/orienting capture.** Any benefit of desaturation has to come from somewhere else (reward/appetitive salience of colour), which is a different literature with different evidence.

## 1. Why editing exists / what it buys the viewer

### Takeaway
Editing works because a well-made cut is placed where perception was *already* about to break — at an event boundary, or at a moment of motion-masked insensitivity. **[MODERATE-to-ROBUST]** The single most compelling empirical demonstration is the first-time-viewer work: people who had never seen film or television in their lives could comprehend edited sequences **as long as a familiar line of action bridged the cut**, which shows continuity editing is not an arbitrary learned code but is keyed to everyday action understanding.

### Cited Findings

**AToCC — its actual claim, verbatim [MODERATE; and partly revised by its own author's later data]**
- AToCC is "a cognitive theory of how continuity is perceived across an edited film sequence that is derived from contemporary theories of real-world scene perception and active vision" — [Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu, *Media Psychology* preprint, p.7](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf), describing Smith (2012a)
- AToCC's cognitive explanation of match-action, quoted in that paper from Smith (2012a, p.15): *"Sudden onsets of movement within a shot draw attention to the screen location of the movement and its future trajectory…. Expectations about the visual scene and object features are abandoned as we focus on the spatiotemporal details of the action. If a match-action cut is timed to coincide with the onset of the action and presents a new viewpoint of the same action at the same screen location as pre-cut the viewers' expectations will be satisfied and a priori continuity will be perceived."* — [ibid., p.7–8](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- **The mechanism is expectation-satisfaction, not attention-grabbing.** This is the key reframe for the report: on AToCC a good cut *satisfies* a prediction the viewer has already formed. It is the opposite of an intrusion.
- **Caveat — AToCC has been criticised and partly revised.** Levin & Hymel (2012) responded to AToCC with evidence that viewers fail to detect *impossible orderings* of actions in an edited sequence (e.g. a screwdriver being used before it was picked up), arguing constant prediction is computationally inefficient and that **postdiction** — perceiving the logical sequence after the fact — is the better mechanism — [ibid., p.27](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf) **[CONTESTED]**
- Smith's own 2016 data led him to propose "a slight modification to AToCC": some cuts, such as match-action cuts, "may not entail the pre-cut formulation of expectations about the future form of events but may instead either create an absence of expectation… or absence of perception due to backward masking" — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf) **[CONTESTED]**

**Event segmentation — does editing align with natural chunking? [MODERATE — yes, but with a twist that matters]**
- AToCC's prediction assumption "was adapted from event segmentation theory (Zacks & Magliano, 2013), in which constant prediction about the short and long term form of events is used to identify moments when certainty in the future drops below an acceptable threshold and a critical boundary between events is perceived" — [ibid., p.27](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Event boundaries "are thought to be accompanied by an increase in attention in order to encode new information and formulate new predictions" (Levin & Saylor, 2008; Zacks & Magliano, 2013) — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Evidence for peak attention at event boundaries comes from **fMRI** (Zacks et al., 2001) and from **eye tracking** showing a peak in attentional synchrony at event boundaries, directed primarily at the actor's **hands** (fine boundaries) or **face** (coarse boundaries) (Smith, 2012c) — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- **The qualification the coordinator asked me to verify carefully, and it is important:** Smith concludes that sudden motion onsets of the kind used in match-action cuts "may sometimes constitute **fine event boundaries** and, therefore indicate a momentary **breakdown in prediction**, contrary to that originally proposed by AToCC" — [ibid., p.27](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- So the honest version of the "editing mirrors natural segmentation" framing is: **a cut placed at an event boundary lands at a moment when the perceptual system was already resetting its model and taking in new information — which is why it costs so little.** That is strong and defensible. What is *not* established is the stronger claim that the cut is therefore "free" or that it is identical to a natural attentional shift. Smith's own data suggest the boundary involves a genuine prediction breakdown that the new shot then fills.

**First-time viewers — the best single piece of benefit evidence [MODERATE, striking]**
- Schwan & Ildirar (2010) showed short film sequences to participants from a remote community in Turkey **who had never previously been exposed to TV or film**. Asked to describe what they had seen, first-time viewers "were only able to accurately describe edited sequences in which a familiar line of action bridged the cut" — [ibid., p.8](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Follow-up (Ildirar & Schwan, 2015, *British Journal of Psychology* 106:133–151, doi:10.1111/bjop.12069) compared cuts combining shots with different relation types (pictorial, e.g. the same person; and inferential, e.g. a character looking down at their shoes shown in close-up, or intending to throw a stone). **First-time viewers were able to perceive all types of relationships but struggled when the activity depicted was not clearly established before the cut, or when a character's intention had to be inferred.** Smith's interpretation: "given their absence of prior knowledge of editing conventions, these results suggest that first-time viewers were using familiarity with the everyday actions depicted in the first shot to guide comprehension across the cut" — [ibid., p.8](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf) (primary paywalled, 403)
- **This is the load-bearing finding for the benefit framing.** Continuity editing is not a learned arbitrary grammar — it works on naive viewers because it is keyed to everyday action comprehension. That is the strongest possible statement that cuts serve the viewer.
- Honest limit noted in the same source: "whether the novice viewers experienced more or less edit blindness than experienced viewers is not known" — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)

**Cuts actively help processing [MODERATE]**
- Per-cut benefits documented in the literature Smith reviews: **improved recognition memory** (Frith & Robson 1975; Lang 1991), **faster recognition for information presented after a cut** (Carroll & Bever 1976, *Science* 191:1053–1055, "Segmentation in Cinema Perception"), and **focusing of attention** (Reeves et al. 1985) — [ibid., p.5](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Note the Carroll & Bever title: the 1976 *Science* paper framed cinema perception as **segmentation** three decades before event segmentation theory. A cut is read by the system as a segment boundary.
- Viewers tolerate outright physical impossibility in service of continuity: participants perceived an **overlap of three frames (~125 ms)** in a match-action cut as continuous, even when the cut went to a shot from the same viewpoint separated by a noise mask (Shimamura, Cohn-Sheehy & Shimamura 2014; see also Hecht & Kalkofen 2009) — [ibid.](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf). The perceptual system is actively *buying* continuity, not resisting the cut.
- Audio as a **"perceptual scaffold"**: audio "is the only property of a film that can remain continuous across a cut and as such it may provide a perceptual 'scaffold' upon which audiovisual continuity can be perceived" — [ibid., p.9](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- A second candidate mechanism, **motion silencing** (Suchow & Alvarez 2011): the inability to detect changes to object hue, luminance, size or shape when they or the background move relative to a stable fixation — [ibid., p.9](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf). **Note for the report: this is another luminance/motion mechanism in which hue change is *silenced*, reinforcing the Claim 2 limitation that colour is not where the action is.**
- Hochberg & Brooks' construct is literally **"Film Cutting and Visual Momentum"** (1978) — cutting as something that *carries* the viewer forward — [ibid., references](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)

### Inferences
- The benefit is best stated as **efficiency purchased at near-zero perceptual cost**: editing lets a narrative skip dead time and change viewpoint ~1,000–2,000 times in 90 minutes while viewers miss a quarter to a third of those transitions entirely and show *better* recognition for post-cut information.
- The report's current one-sided framing is not just unbalanced, it is mechanistically backwards: on AToCC a well-placed cut *satisfies* an expectation rather than violating one.

### Gaps
- **No head-to-head edited-vs-single-take comprehension experiment found.** I searched for this in the previous round and could not fetch one this round (budget exhausted). The first-time-viewer studies are the closest available evidence and they are *within*-edited comparisons (which shot relations work), not edited-vs-unedited. **Do not assert "edited sequences are comprehended better than unedited ones" — I have no source for that comparison.**
- **No research on ellipsis per se** (omission of dead time) located. The inference-across-cuts evidence I have is Ildirar & Schwan on shot relations and Levin & Hymel on postdiction. The specific claim "editing lets minutes convey what real time would take hours" is **[UNSUPPORTED]** as an empirical finding — it is a true observation about film form, not a measured result. State it as the former.

## 2. The inverted-U, stated as precisely as the sources allow

### Takeaway
**The direction is reliable; the precise numbers are not obtainable from the sources I could reach [MODERATE on direction, GAP on quantification].** And the key moderator turns out to be **message complexity**, not edit rate alone. Crucially for the report: **heart-rate deceleration indicates resource *intake*, not stress** — getting this wrong would badly mislead readers.

### Cited Findings

**Heart rate: deceleration = orienting = intake [ROBUST in this literature]**
- "Heart rate deceleration serves as a physiological marker of orienting responses (automatic cognitive reactions to novel stimuli)." The pattern is that "their heart rate decelerates for a few seconds before returning toward their baseline," combined with increased skin conductance indicating arousal and engagement — [LC4MP, Wikipedia summary of the model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- Cuts produce heart-rate **deceleration** (Lang 1990) and increased self-reported arousal (Lang et al. 2000) — [Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu preprint, p.5](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- Lang's dissertation title confirms cardiac response as the core measure: *"The effects of the formal features of television on viewers' attention and arousal: Cardiac response, attention, and arousal"* — [Annie Lang, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Lang)
- **How to state this correctly:** in media psychophysiology, cardiac *deceleration* accompanies attentional intake of external information (the orienting response), whereas *acceleration* accompanies defensive/rejection states and internal cognitive load. So "cuts decelerate the heart" means **cuts open the gate for taking information in** — it is an engagement signature, not a stress signature. The report must not imply cuts are physiologically stressful; the measured response is the opposite pattern. **[Note: the intake–rejection framing is Lacey's, and I could NOT retrieve a Lacey primary source or a source that explicitly names Lacey this round — the deceleration=orienting equation above IS sourced, the attribution to Lacey's intake-rejection hypothesis is my own identification of the framework and should be verified before being named in print.]**

**The inverted-U and its real moderator [MODERATE]**
- As edit rate increases, "physiological arousal, self-reported arousal, and memory increase" — [Lang et al., *The Effects of Edits on Arousal, Attention, and Memory for Television Messages: When an Edit Is an Edit Can an Edit Be Too Much?*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249025611_The_Effects_of_Edits_on_Arousal_Attention_and_Memory_for_Television_Messages_When_an_Edit_Is_an_Edit_Can_an_Edit_Be_Too_Much) **(abstract-level only; full text 403 again this round)**
- Both fast pace and arousing content elicit self-reported arousal but different patterns of physiological arousal, and both increase resource allocation — **but the combination of fast pacing and arousing content can overwhelm capacity, decreasing content recognition and recall** — [Lang, production pacing and arousing content](https://www.academia.edu/2914177/The_effects_of_production_pacing_and_arousing_content_on_the_information_processing_of_television_messages) **(abstract-level; 403 on full text)**
- **The moderator is message complexity**, which is more useful than a bare rate threshold: "For simple messages, attention-directing edits improve memory. For complex messages, those same edits paradoxically worsen memory performance, suggesting resources were diverted away from encoding" — [LC4MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- Related vs unrelated edits differ fundamentally: "Related editing in camera angle and content" vs "unrelated cuts to unrelated information" produce different cognitive effects — "related edits reinforce attention and memory, while rapid, unrelated cuts fail to encode into memory" — [LC4MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- Resource accounting: the model distinguishes total, allocated and required resources; "resources are automatically drawn by formal features like cuts, potentially depleting capacity for encoding" — [LC4MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- Memory is indexed by recognition (encoding), cued recall (storage) and free recall (retrieval) — these can dissociate, so "memory" claims must specify which — [LC4MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- As degree of change at a cut increases, so do both viewer awareness of the cut and cognitive load (Lang, Kurita, Gao & Rubenking 2013) — [Smith preprint, p.5](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)

### Gaps
- **No published numerical optimum.** I could not retrieve Lang's tested edit rates (in cuts/minute or seconds/shot) or any stated optimal range — three separate attempts across ResearchGate, Academia.edu and Google Scholar all returned 403, in both research rounds. **The report must NOT state a numeric optimum or threshold.** State the shape and the moderators; do not invent a number.
- I could not confirm whether Lang's edit-rate study used a continuous manipulation (permitting a genuine curve) or two/three discrete levels (permitting only "more vs less"). This materially affects whether "inverted-U" is even the right description. **Prefer "benefit up to a point, cost when combined with arousing or complex content" over "inverted-U", which implies a measured curve I cannot document.**

## 3. When does it tip from benefit to cost?

### Takeaway
**Not rate alone [MODERATE].** The documented discriminators are (a) rate × arousing content, (b) message complexity, (c) whether edits are *related* or *unrelated*, and (d) whether the cut respects continuity conventions. Each has at least abstract-level support. Rate in isolation mostly *helps*.

### Cited Findings
- **Rate × arousing content**: the combination overwhelms capacity and decreases recognition and recall; neither alone does — [Lang, pacing and arousing content](https://www.academia.edu/2914177/The_effects_of_production_pacing_and_arousing_content_on_the_information_processing_of_television_messages)
- **Complexity**: attention-directing edits improve memory for simple messages and worsen it for complex ones — [LC4MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- **Relatedness**: related edits reinforce attention and memory; rapid unrelated cuts fail to encode — [LC4MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- **Convention violation**: d'Ydewalle et al. (1998) found a peak in saccadic activity following cuts that **violated** the 180° rule — the visual system reacts to badly constructed cuts, not to cuts as such; and Frith & Robson (1975) found children reconstructed rule-compliant films more accurately — [Smith, SAGE Encyclopedia preprint](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21202/2/f4fb8655-0e0a-461f-8852-163a38bd629c.pdf)
- **Magnitude of change**: as the degree of change at a cut increases, awareness and cognitive load both rise (Lang et al. 2013); cuts between *unrelated scenes* were missed only 9.4% of the time vs 32.4% for match-action — [Smith preprint](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)
- **Event-boundary alignment**: cuts that land at event boundaries land where attention was already rising to encode new information — [Smith preprint, p.27](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf)

### Does short-form platform video differ STRUCTURALLY? — **[UNSUPPORTED]**
This is the distinction the report wants, and **I must report that no evidence supports it.**
- There is **no measurement of cut rates, shot lengths, or continuity-convention adherence in TikTok/Reels/Shorts** — confirmed across both research rounds. Film shot lengths are well measured (Cutting; Bordwell & Thompson); short-form is not measured at all.
- There is **no study comparing platform short-form video to narrative film on any structural-editing dimension**, and none testing whether platform cuts are "unmotivated" or lack continuity scaffolding.
- The theoretically motivated hypotheses *do* exist in a usable form — Lang's related-vs-unrelated edit distinction, and the fact that each new clip in a feed is a genuine scene change (which the Smith data say is the *most* noticeable cut type, 9.4% miss rate, i.e. least hidden). **A feed of unrelated clips is structurally closer to the "unrelated cuts" condition that fails to encode than to continuity-edited film.** But that is an inference by analogy from adjacent findings, not a measured result about these platforms.
- **Recommendation:** the report may state this as an explicitly flagged hypothesis ("one would expect…, though no one has measured it"), or omit it. It must not be stated as a finding. If the report wants to be critical of short-form video on structural grounds, **the evidence base for that criticism does not currently exist.**

## 4. Honest bottom line

**Two-sided statement, balanced:**

> **Benefit [MODERATE-to-ROBUST]:** Cutting is a perceptual technology that works with the grain of the visual system — placed at a moment when motion masks the transition and the viewer's model of the event was already resetting, a cut costs so little that viewers miss roughly a third of them even when paid to spot them, and recognise information presented *after* a cut faster than information before it; it works even on adults who have never seen a film in their lives, provided a familiar action bridges the cut.
>
> **Cost [MODERATE, narrower than usually claimed]:** Raising edit rate raises arousal and resource allocation, and when high rate combines with arousing or complex content, or when cuts are unrelated to one another, capacity is exceeded and recognition and recall decline.
