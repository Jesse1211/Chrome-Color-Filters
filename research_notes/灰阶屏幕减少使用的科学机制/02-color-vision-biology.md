# The Biology and Neuroscience of Human Color Vision — Why Color Is More Arousing/Salient Than Grayscale

> Scope note for the report-writer: findings below are tagged **[ROBUST]**, **[CONTESTED]**, or **[UNVERIFIED]**.
> Two numbers from the research brief needed correction and are flagged in the first section.
> The single most important structural caveat for the whole topic: the retinal/pathway anatomy is
> rock-solid, the attention/visual-search story is rock-solid, and the "color → arousal" story is
> *moderately* supported but sits next to a large body of color-psychology work with known
> replication failures. Do not let the anatomy's certainty bleed into the affect claims.

## Q1. Retinal hardware with numbers: rods vs. cones, L/M/S ratio, S-cone sparsity, fovea vs. periphery

### Takeaway
The canonical counts are **92 million rods and 4.6 million cones** (Curcio et al. 1990), not the
"120M/6M" figures in common circulation — those older numbers come from Østerberg (1935)-era
single-retina estimates and should be corrected in the explainer. S-cones are genuinely rare
(8–12% of cones, absent from the very center of the foveola), and the L:M ratio is *highly*
variable between individuals (population range ~1:4 to 16.5:1, mean ≈2:1), which means
"63:31:6" is a defensible central tendency but must not be presented as a fixed constant.

### Cited Findings
- **Rods: 92 million (range 77.9–107.3 million). Cones: 4.6 million (range 4.08–5.29 million).** Direct quote from the abstract: "The average human retina contains 4.6 million cones (4.08–5.29 million)… The average human retina contains 92 million rods (77.9–107.3 million)." Based on 8 whole-mounted retinas from 7 donors aged 27–44. — [Curcio, Sloan, Kalina & Hendrickson, *J Comp Neurol* 292:497–523 (1990)](https://christineacurcio.com/PRtopo/Curcio_JCompNeurol1990_PRtopo_searchable.pdf) **[ROBUST — this is the field's standard citation]**
- ⚠️ **CORRECTION TO BRIEF:** the brief's "~120M rods, ~6M cones" is the pre-1990 textbook figure. Curcio's 92M/4.6M supersedes it. If the explainer wants round numbers, use "~92 million rods, ~4.6 million cones" or "roughly 90M vs 5M." Overall rod:cone ≈ **20:1** across the whole retina, varying from ~1:1 near the foveal center to ~30:1 in the periphery. — [Frontiers in Cell & Dev Biol 10:878350 (2022) review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cell-and-developmental-biology/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.878350/full)
- **Peak foveal cone density: 199,000 cones/mm², highly variable (100,000–324,000 cones/mm²)** — a threefold between-individual range. — [Curcio et al. 1990](https://christineacurcio.com/PRtopo/Curcio_JCompNeurol1990_PRtopo_searchable.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **Cone density falls an order of magnitude within 1 mm of the foveal center**, and reaches ~2,000 cones/mm² at 5–16 mm eccentricity. So roughly 99% of cones sit outside the high-acuity center, but the center is where the density is concentrated by two orders of magnitude. — [Curcio et al. 1990](https://christineacurcio.com/PRtopo/Curcio_JCompNeurol1990_PRtopo_searchable.pdf); peripheral figure via [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cell-and-developmental-biology/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.878350/full) **[ROBUST]**
- **Rod-free zone in the fovea: average horizontal diameter 0.350 mm ≈ 1.25° of visual angle** (adult range reported elsewhere as 350–720 μm). The very center of gaze is therefore *cone-only* — pure color/detail hardware, no rods. — [Curcio et al. 1990](https://christineacurcio.com/PRtopo/Curcio_JCompNeurol1990_PRtopo_searchable.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **Rods peak in an elliptical ring at roughly the eccentricity of the optic disc (~20°)**, then decline slowly to the far periphery. — [Curcio et al. 1990](https://christineacurcio.com/PRtopo/Curcio_JCompNeurol1990_PRtopo_searchable.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **Nasal/temporal asymmetry: at equivalent eccentricities, cone density is 40–45% higher in nasal than temporal retina.** — [Curcio et al. 1990](https://christineacurcio.com/PRtopo/Curcio_JCompNeurol1990_PRtopo_searchable.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **S-cones are 8–12% of the cone population and this proportion is roughly consistent across the retina**; the L+M : S ratio is about **100:1** in some regional estimates. S-cone fraction **increases nearly monotonically with eccentricity from 2° to 12°**. — [Frontiers 2022 review, citing de Monasterio et al. 1985 and Curcio et al. 1991](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cell-and-developmental-biology/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.878350/full); eccentricity trend via [Hofer et al., *J Neurosci* 25(42):9669 "Organization of the Human Trichromatic Cone Mosaic"](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/jneuro/25/42/9669.full.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **L:M ratio: population range 1:4 to 16.5:1, average ≈2:1** (i.e., roughly twice as many L as M cones on average). Independent statement of the same thing: "on average about 2:1, but varies between individuals from about 1:1 to at least as high as 6:1." — [Frontiers 2022, citing Yamaguchi et al. 1997 and Hofer et al. 2005](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cell-and-developmental-biology/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.878350/full); [Bowmaker, Parry & Mollon (2003), "The Arrangement of L and M Cones in Human and a Primate Retina"](http://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/BowmakerParryMollon2003.pdf) **[ROBUST on the mean; the variance is the headline finding]**
- ⚠️ **PARTIAL CORRECTION TO BRIEF:** "L:M:S = 63:31:6" is consistent with a ~2:1 L:M and ~6% S, so it is a reasonable *central* estimate and is widely quoted. But it must be labeled as an average, because the L:M component varies by more than an order of magnitude across normal trichromats (Hofer et al. 2005 found observers with near-total M-cone absence who still had normal color vision). Presenting 63:31:6 as "the" human ratio would be misleading.
- **Why S-cones are sparse — mechanistic reasons:** (a) the eye's optics are chromatically aberrant, and short wavelengths are defocused most, so a dense S-cone array would sample a blurred image and gain nothing; (b) the S-cone system therefore doesn't need to carry fine spatial detail, and the S-channel's spatial resolution is correspondingly poor; (c) S-cones are absent from the central ~0.3–0.4° *foveola* ("S-cone-free zone"), meaning the very point of fixation is tritanopic. — the optical/sampling argument is developed in [Garrigan et al., "Design of a Trichromatic Cone Array," *PLoS Comput Biol* (2010)](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1000677) **[ROBUST as a consensus explanation; the specific "foveal tritanopia" radius is better verified from Curcio et al. 1991 which I could not fetch directly — see Gaps]**
- **Color vision becomes effectively dichromatic (loses L−M cone opponency) at eccentricities beyond about 25–30°.** Red-green color is a *central-vision* capability. — [Hansen, Pracejus & Gegenfurtner, "Color perception in the intermediate periphery of the visual field," *J Vision* (2009)](https://www.allpsych.uni-giessen.de/hansen/publications/HansenPracejusGegenfurtner2009JVision.pdf) **[ROBUST]**

### Inferences
- The architecture is *foveally* color-biased: the point of gaze is a rod-free, S-cone-free, all-L/M patch with ~200,000 cones/mm². This is the hardware basis for "color grabs the center of gaze" — the L−M channel is at maximum fidelity exactly where the eye is pointed, and degrades to nothing by 30° out.
- The enormous individual variation in L:M ratio with near-normal color *experience* implies the brain normalizes the chromatic channels against the statistics of the environment — relevant to the explainer because it means "color salience" is a learned/calibrated response to habitual input, not a fixed wiring constant. (This is an inference; see Webster's work on chromatic adaptation for the direct evidence, which I did not fetch.)

### Gaps
- Could not directly fetch Curcio et al. (1991) *J Comp Neurol* "Distribution and morphology of human cone photoreceptors stained with anti-blue opsin," which is the primary source for the S-cone-free foveal zone diameter. The 8–12% S-cone figure is attributed to it via the 2022 Frontiers review, which I did verify.
- Did not verify an exact numerical value for the diameter of the S-cone-free foveola (figures of ~0.3–0.4° / ~100 μm circulate but I could not confirm against primary source). **Flag as unverified if used.**

## Q2. Opponent-process coding: L−M, S−(L+M), and the luminance channel — which carries spatial detail?

### Takeaway
The retina re-codes three cone signals into three post-receptoral channels: an achromatic
**luminance** channel (L+M), a **red-green** channel (**L−M**), and a **blue-yellow** channel
(**S−(L+M)**). The luminance channel carries essentially all the fine spatial detail — its
resolution extends to ~30 cycles/deg while the chromatic channels cut off at ~11–12 cycles/deg,
and chromatic contrast sensitivity is **low-pass** where luminance is **band-pass**.

### Cited Findings
- **Channel architecture:** the S-vs-(L+M) comparison is mediated by a small population of **small bistratified ganglion cells** projecting to the **koniocellular** LGN layers, constituting "a neural channel that is thought to remain largely separate from other visual pathways." The L-vs-M comparison is mediated by the **parvocellular** system, "which is present in all primates and is thought to subserve the resolution of spatial detail." "Together these S/L and L/M subsystems create trichromacy." — [Sumner & Mollon, "Did primate trichromacy evolve for frugivory or folivory?", in *Normal and Defective Colour Vision* (OUP), Ch. 3, pp. 21–30](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/SumnerMollonFrugivory.pdf) **[ROBUST — direct quote from primary chapter text]**
- **Chromatic CSFs are low-pass; luminance CSF is band-pass.** "The spatial filtering applied by the human visual system appears to be low pass for chromatic stimuli and band pass for luminance stimuli." The luminance modulation transfer function has a pass band roughly **2–8 cycles/deg**. — [Ferreira & Rodrigues / semanticscholar, "Are Spatial Chromatic Contrast Sensitivity Band-pass or Low-pass Functions?"](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/b2c5/9b2f827c96b89154c76332b2f3620a7bae7e.pdf) **[ROBUST — the low-pass/band-pass distinction is textbook consensus]**
- **Resolution limits — the key number pair.** Mullen (1985), the canonical measurement: "Both blue-yellow and red-green contrast sensitivity functions have similar low-pass characteristics… The limiting acuities based on red-green and blue-yellow colour discriminations are similar at **11 or 12 cycles/deg**." By contrast, **luminance modulation is visible up to ~30 cycles/deg**, and isoluminant red/green chrominance modulation is not seen above about **12 cycles/deg**. — [Mullen, K.T. (1985), *J Physiol* 359:381–400](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/jphysiol.1985.sp015591) **[ROBUST — Mullen 1985 is *the* citation for this; ~1,000s of citations]**
- **At low spatial frequencies (below ~0.5 cycles/deg) chromatic sensitivity is *greater* than luminance sensitivity** to the component gratings. So the chromatic channels are not globally weaker — they dominate at coarse spatial scales (large colored regions), exactly the scale of UI elements, app icons, thumbnails and notification badges. — [Mullen (1985)](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/jphysiol.1985.sp015591) **[ROBUST]**
- **Red-green has higher sensitivity / extends to higher spatial frequencies than blue-yellow** among the two chromatic CSFs. — [summary of chromatic CSF literature](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/b2c5/9b2f827c96b89154c76332b2f3620a7bae7e.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **Midget ganglion cells in the peripheral retina (20–50°) remain strongly cone-opponent**, and only ~2–3% of parafoveal midget cells are blue-OFF. — [Dacey, *Annu Rev Neurosci* 23:743–775 (2000), "Parallel pathways for spectral coding in primate retina"](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dmacleod/221/color%20papers/daceyreview.pdf) **[ROBUST]**

### Inferences
- **This is the crux of the mechanism for the explainer.** Grayscale removes the L−M and S−(L+M) channels entirely while leaving the luminance channel intact. Because luminance carries the high-spatial-frequency detail, grayscale costs you almost *no* legibility — text, edges, layout, and icons remain readable at full acuity. But because chromatic channels dominate at *low* spatial frequencies (<0.5 c/deg), grayscale strips precisely the large-area, "what jumps out from across the room" signal. That asymmetry is why grayscale is a nearly free intervention: you lose the salience/hedonic layer and keep the information layer.
- Red-green (L−M) is the strongest chromatic channel, it is the evolutionarily newest, and it's the one tuned to ripeness and skin (see Q6). Saturated reds and oranges in app icons and notification badges are therefore hitting the single highest-gain chromatic channel the visual system has.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the full text of Gegenfurtner & Kiper, *Annu Rev Neurosci* 26:181–206 (2003) "Color vision" (the PDF at cns.nyu.edu resolved to front matter only). Its claims are standard and well-corroborated by the sources above, but if the report wants a single review citation for the opponent-channel architecture, that is the one to cite — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12574494/).

## Q3. Magnocellular vs. parvocellular vs. koniocellular: speed, contrast sensitivity, and color

### Takeaway
**The brief's key point is confirmed:** the magnocellular pathway is faster (~10 ms lead at the LGN),
has much higher achromatic contrast sensitivity, and is essentially color-blind; the parvocellular
pathway carries the L−M chromatic signal with low contrast sensitivity and high spatial resolution;
koniocellular carries S-cone (blue-yellow) signals and is the slowest (~10 ms *behind* parvo).

### Cited Findings
- **Cell-type proportions:** midget/parvocellular cells are **~80% of foveal ganglion cells**; parasol/magnocellular cells are **~10%**. At the LGN, "magno- and parvocellular neurons account for 90% of all LGN relay cells, with the remaining 10% belonging to the koniocellular pathway." — [Dacey, *Annu Rev Neurosci* (2000)](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dmacleod/221/color%20papers/daceyreview.pdf); LGN proportions via [Wikipedia LGN article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_geniculate_nucleus) **[ROBUST on retinal proportions (Dacey); the "90%/10%" LGN split is a commonly repeated figure that I verified only against a secondary source — flag as approximate]**
- **Contrast sensitivity:** "Magnocellular cells had much higher contrast sensitivities than did parvocellular cells." "Most parvocellular geniculate cells are color selective and have low contrast sensitivity, whereas magnocellular cells are generally not color selective and have higher contrast sensitivity." — [Kaplan & Shapley and successors; see the J Neurosci "Color and Contrast" literature](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/jneuro/10/7/2223.full.pdf) **[ROBUST — one of the most replicated facts in primate visual physiology]**
- **Latency:** "Over a wide range of stimulus intensities the fastest magnocellular response latencies preceded the fastest parvocellular response latencies by about **10 ms**." — [Maunsell et al., "Visual response latencies of magnocellular and parvocellular LGN neurons in macaque monkeys," *Visual Neuroscience* (1999)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/13265977_Visual_response_latencies_of_magnocellular_and_parvocellular_LGN_neurons_in_macaque_monkeys) **[ROBUST]**
- **Koniocellular latency:** "Koniocellular neurons have response latencies about **10 ms longer** than those of parvocellular neurons." — [Cheong, Pietersen, Solomon, Martin et al., "Antidromic latency of magnocellular, parvocellular, and koniocellular (Blue-ON) geniculocortical relay cells in marmosets"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261406602_Antidromic_latency_of_magnocellular_parvocellular_and_koniocellular_Blue-ON_geniculocortical_relay_cells_in_marmosets) **[ROBUST]**
- **Functional division:** "Parvocellular cells are more sensitive to chromatic properties of a stimulus, and magnocellular cells to rapid changes in achromatic contrast and luminance modulation. Parvo-cells have smaller receptive fields and their contrast sensitivity is comparatively low, however, many of them are sensitive to color (wavelength)." — [Kaplan/Shapley-lineage literature as above](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/jneuro/10/7/2223.full.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **Important nuance / complication:** in owl monkey, "koniocellular contrast sensitivity and temporal frequency tuning are similar to those of parvocellular cells, and they receive negligible functional input from short wavelength-sensitive ('blue') cone photoreceptors" — i.e., the konio=blue-yellow mapping is species-dependent and not a clean one-to-one. — [Xu, Kaas et al., "A comparison of koniocellular, magnocellular and parvocellular receptive field properties in the LGN of the owl monkey"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12152271_A_comparison_of_koniocellular_magnocellular_and_parvocellular_receptive_field_properties_in_the_lateral_geniculate_nucleus_of_the_owl_monkey_Aotus_trivirgatus) **[ROBUST finding, but note owl monkeys are nocturnal and atypical; in macaque/marmoset the Blue-ON konio cells do carry S-cone signals]**
- The strict magno=where/parvo=what dichotomy has been substantially complicated: parvo and magno channels **converge in V1**. The pathways are not independent all the way up. — [Convergence of parvocellular and magnocellular channels in the primary visual cortex of the macaque](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11087853_Convergence_of_parvocellular_and_magnocellular_channels_in_the_primary_visual_cortex_of_the_macaque) **[ROBUST — important caveat against overselling the "two separate brains" story]**

### Inferences
- The timing story is the one worth putting in the explainer: **luminance information arrives first (~10 ms ahead), chromatic information arrives second.** This predicts that grayscale does NOT slow down the fast, reflexive "something changed / something moved" detection — magno is intact. What grayscale removes is the slightly later-arriving chromatic signal that does the *identifying* and *valuing* ("that's the red badge," "that's the thumbnail of food"). This fits the behavioral picture: grayscale doesn't make phones harder to use, it makes them less worth picking up.
- Parvo being ~80% of ganglion cells means roughly four-fifths of the optic nerve's cells are in the chromatically-opponent, high-resolution channel. Grayscale does not silence those cells — they still signal luminance edges — but it collapses their chromatic dimension, removing one of the two things each midget cell encodes.

### Gaps
- Could not find a quantitative figure for *how much* contrast sensitivity differs (e.g., a ratio) from a primary source in this search round; the literature typically reports magno sensitivity as roughly an order of magnitude higher at low contrasts (Kaplan & Shapley 1986), but **I did not verify a specific multiplier — flag as unverified if a number is wanted.**

## Q4. Color and attention: color as a preattentive / guiding feature

### Takeaway
Color is one of a very short list of features that reliably "pop out" — it produces near-flat
search slopes (search time independent of the number of distractors), and in the modern
authoritative taxonomy (Wolfe & Horowitz 2017) color is one of only four "undoubted" attributes
that guide attention. Luminance contrast is demoted to the second tier of merely "probable"
guides. That ranking is the strongest single piece of evidence for the brief's thesis.

### Cited Findings
- **The headline ranking:** "**color, motion, orientation, and size** are classified as **'undoubted' guides** of attention because target–distractor differences on these dimensions reliably produce efficient (near-flat-slope) search across many studies. A second tier of **'probable' guides** included **luminance contrast**, depth, and shape." — [Wolfe & Horowitz, "Five factors that guide attention in visual search," *Nature Human Behaviour* 1:0058 (2017)](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/FiveFactors_Wolfe-Horowitz_2017.pdf) **[ROBUST — this is a Nature Hum Behav review by the field's leading authority, and the ranking of color above luminance is explicit]**
- **Attention is guided by five factors:** "bottom-up salience, top-down feature guidance, scene structure and meaning, the previous history of search over timescales ranging from milliseconds to years, and the relative value of the targets and distractors." Note that **"relative value of the targets"** is itself one of the five — directly relevant to the reward framing of phone use. — [Wolfe & Horowitz (2017)](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/FiveFactors_Wolfe-Horowitz_2017.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **Feature Integration Theory:** Treisman posited "an initial, 'preattentive' stage of processing, in which a limited set of basic features like color and orientation could be processed in parallel across the visual field." Feature search (target differing in one feature, e.g., color) is fast and parallel; conjunction search requires serial attention. Color is the textbook example of a preattentive feature producing pop-out. — [Treisman & Souther, *JEP: General* 114(3) (1985)](https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/379169/mod_resource/content/0/TreismanSouther1985.pdf); [Treisman (1982), "Perceptual grouping and attention in visual search for features and for objects"](http://wexler.free.fr/library/files/treisman%20(1982)%20perceptual%20grouping%20and%20attention%20in%20visual%20search%20for%20features%20and%20for%20objects.pdf) **[ROBUST as a phenomenon; FIT's *two-stage architecture* has been superseded by Guided Search 6.0, see below]**
- **FIT superseded but the color finding survives:** the modern model is Guided Search 6.0, which replaces the strict parallel/serial dichotomy with a continuous guidance signal — but color remains one of its strongest guiding dimensions. — [Wolfe, "Guided Search 6.0: An updated model of visual search," *Psychon Bull Rev* (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01859-9) **[ROBUST]**
- **Not all colors guide equally:** attentional guidance by individual colors varies, and guidance degrades as the distractor set becomes more heterogeneous. — [Andersen & Maier (2019), "The attentional guidance of individual colours in increasingly complex displays"](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/78665/1/Andersen_Maier_AE_2019_The_attentional_guidance_of_individual_colours_in_increasingly.pdf) **[ROBUST — useful caveat: a red badge pops out against a uniform grey UI far more than against a rainbow of other icons]**
- **Counterweight — meaning beats low-level salience in real scenes:** in real-world scene viewing, semantic "meaning maps" predict fixations as well as or better than low-level saliency. — [Henderson & Hayes, "Meaning Guides Attention in Real-World Scene Images" (bioRxiv/Nature Hum Behav)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/207076v2.full.pdf) **[ROBUST and important: do not claim color salience is the dominant driver of gaze in naturalistic viewing — it is one driver among several, and meaning is at least as strong]**

### Inferences
- The Wolfe & Horowitz tiering is the cleanest way to state the mechanism for a lay explainer: *color is a first-tier attention magnet; brightness is only second-tier.* Converting a screen to grayscale therefore downgrades every element on it from a first-tier to a second-tier attentional signal, while leaving all the information intact.
- Because guidance degrades with distractor heterogeneity (Andersen & Maier), grayscale has a second-order effect: in a full-color UI, a red badge competes against many other colors; but the *reason* color works so well in app design is that designers deliberately keep backgrounds desaturated so the colored call-to-action is a unique-feature singleton. Grayscale destroys the singleton by removing the dimension it was unique in.

### Gaps
- I did not find a clean quantitative figure of the form "color search slope = X ms/item vs. luminance = Y ms/item" from a primary source in this round. Wolfe's lab data typically shows near-zero slopes (<5 ms/item) for strong color singletons versus ~20–40 ms/item for serial search, but **I could not verify specific slope values — flag as unverified if numbers are needed.**

## Q5. Computational saliency models: how much weight do color channels get, and what does desaturation do?

### Takeaway
In the standard Itti & Koch architecture, color gets **two of the three feature families** (two
color-opponent channels vs. one intensity channel, plus four orientation channels), so color is
structurally weighted heavily by construction. But the honest finding is that *measured* color
weight in fixation prediction is modest and the literature is mixed, and the Itti-Koch model is
now substantially outperformed by deep models — so saliency-model weights are weak evidence
for "color drives gaze." Behavioral desaturation evidence is also weaker than one would expect.

### Cited Findings
- **Model architecture:** "The Itti–Koch model includes **two color channels (blue/yellow and red/green), one intensity channel, and four orientation channels (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°)**." Color contrast is computed via chromatic **double-opponency** (red-on-green, blue-on-yellow). The image is decomposed across a multi-scale Gaussian pyramid, with center-surround differences within each channel, normalized and summed into one saliency map. — [Itti & Koch model as described in "Salience Models: A Computational Cognitive Neuroscience Review," *Vision* 3(4):56](https://doi.org/10.3390/vision3040056) **[ROBUST]**
- **Performance ceiling / caveat:** the original Itti-Koch model achieves **AUC-Judd ≈ 0.6** vs **≈0.88** for deep-CNN saliency models. The classic feature channels are therefore a weak account of actual human fixation. — [State-of-the-Art in Human Scanpath Prediction (arXiv 2102.12239)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.12239) **[ROBUST — crucial caveat]**
- **Relative channel contributions are contested:** "Edges and contrast are more related to fixations than luminance," with other work separately quantifying color's contribution — i.e., the ranking among low-level channels is not settled. — [Learning a saliency map using fixated locations in natural scenes, *J Vision*](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2191889) **[CONTESTED]**
- **Psychophysical isolation of individual low-level features** has been attempted directly, which is the right design for this question. — [Psychophysical evaluation of individual low-level feature influences on visual attention (arXiv 1811.06458)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.06458) **[I did not fetch the full text — see Gaps]**
- **⚠️ The strongest contrary evidence the report must include:** in active real-world vision, **observers routinely fail to notice drastic desaturation at all.** In gaze-contingent rendering where the entire visual periphery was desaturated and only the fixated region was in color, "a large number of observers were completely unaware of drastic color manipulations including desaturation," despite fixating long enough to identify and describe the scenes. — [Cohen, Botch & Robertson, "The limits of color awareness during active, real-world vision," *PNAS* 117(24) (2020)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1922294117) **[ROBUST — this is a well-designed PNAS study and it argues against a naive "color is what we see" story. It does NOT refute the salience account (peripheral color awareness ≠ foveal color-driven value), but an honest explainer must not claim color is constantly, consciously dominant.]**
- **Saturation manipulation *does* steer gaze when applied as a gradient:** increasing saturation in a target region and decreasing it elsewhere produces a measurable eye-guidance effect. — [Gaze Guidance in the Real-world by Changing Color Saturation of Objects (Eurographics)](https://diglib.eg.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/ad7d6fc3-e246-462e-9a70-0dff20620719/content) **[MODERATE — conference paper, small-N gaze-guidance literature]**
- "Color saturation, color brightness, and color fidelity all influence how participants view images." — same gaze-guidance literature as above. **[MODERATE]**

### Inferences
- The saliency-model argument should be used *structurally*, not quantitatively: "when engineers build a model of what grabs the eye, two of its seven feature channels are color-opponent channels copied from the retina" is a fair and vivid statement. "Color accounts for N% of fixation prediction" is **not** a defensible claim from what I found.
- The PNAS color-awareness result and the Wolfe & Horowitz guidance result are not in conflict, and the explainer should say why: color guides *where attention goes* and what a thing is *worth*, without being continuously *consciously represented* across the visual field. Grayscale works on the first, not the second — which is also why users report that grayscale "stops being noticeable" after a day or two while still reducing use.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly measures **fixation distributions on the same images presented in color vs. fully desaturated**, with quantitative agreement metrics. This is the single most relevant missing experiment for the brief's thesis. The gaze-guidance papers manipulate *relative* saturation within an image, which is a different question from global desaturation. **Flag clearly as a gap.**

## Q6. Evolutionary origins: the trichromacy hypothesis — frugivory, folivory, or skin signaling?

### Takeaway
The debate is genuinely unresolved and the current consensus is pluralist: the L−M channel is
well-optimized for *both* ripe fruit and young reddish leaves against mature foliage, and
separately for skin/blood signals. The crucial quantitative fact for the explainer: in Sumner &
Mollon's field spectroradiometry, **the red-green channel beat luminance by ~7× for detecting
edible leaves and ~14× for detecting ripe fruit** — the L−M channel exists specifically because
it detects *nutrient and ripeness signals* that the luminance channel cannot.

### Cited Findings
- **The numbers — this is the best quantitative result in this section.** For data collected in Kibale, Uganda (canopy, cloudy conditions), "the signal-to-noise ratio for detecting edible leaves against mature leaves… was about **seven times higher** using L/(L+M) than luminance (L+M or simply L for a dichromat), and for detecting ripe fruits against mature leaves it was about **14 times higher**." — [Sumner & Mollon, *Normal and Defective Colour Vision*, Ch. 3](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/SumnerMollonFrugivory.pdf) **[ROBUST — direct quote from the chapter; authors note exact values depend on species sampling, illumination type, and the dichromat's L-cone λmax]**
- **Ripeness is directly encoded in the L−M signal:** "for most species of consumed fruit in Kibale, the force required to puncture the skin of samples of different maturity (our measure of ripeness) was **inversely related to their L/(L+M) chromaticity values**." I.e., redder = softer = riper. — [Sumner & Mollon, Ch. 3, citing Sumner & Mollon 2000b](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/SumnerMollonFrugivory.pdf) **[ROBUST — direct primary measurement]**
- **The frugivory case:** Regan et al. measured fruit spectra, leaf-background spectra, and forest illumination for three platyrrhine species in French Guiana, and by modeling signal-to-noise for all possible L-pigment pairs found that the photopigments of *Alouatta seniculus* and the trichromatic individuals of *Ateles paniscus* and *Cebus apella* "are well optimized for detecting the fruit in the monkeys' diets against the natural background of forest leaves." — [Sumner & Mollon Ch. 3, summarizing Regan et al. 1998, 2001](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/SumnerMollonFrugivory.pdf); original: [Regan et al., "Fruits, foliage and the evolution of primate colour vision," *Phil Trans R Soc B* (2001)](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/Regan(2001)PTRSB.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **The folivory case:** Lucas et al. (1998) proposed the advantage lay in detecting red/reddish-brown young leaves. Dominy & Lucas (2001) concluded "leaf consumption [has] unique value in maintaining trichromacy in catarrhines," that "the fruit-feeding hypothesis for trichromacy in higher primates is unconvincing," and that "full trichromatic vision evolved originally for leaf foraging in higher primates." — [quoted in Sumner & Mollon Ch. 3](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/SumnerMollonFrugivory.pdf) **[This is a real, strongly-stated position in the literature]**
- **Sumner & Mollon's rebuttal (important for epistemic honesty):** they argue Dominy & Lucas's key argument confounds "the discriminability of two overlapping statistical distributions" with "the discriminability of individual elements drawn from those distributions," and that because fruit is far rarer than leaves in a primate's visual field, a Bayesian prior argument means the S/(L+M) channel alone cannot find fruit. Their own conclusion: "the L/M subsystem provides an advantage for detecting **both** fruits and young leaves amongst mature foliage, and furthermore… the spectral positions of the primates' pigments are **optimized for both tasks**." — [Sumner & Mollon Ch. 3](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/SumnerMollonFrugivory.pdf) **[CONTESTED debate; Sumner & Mollon's pluralist conclusion is the most defensible position]**
- **Independent convergent evidence for folivory:** howler monkeys (*Alouatta*, the one platyrrhine genus with routine trichromacy, which evolved it independently of catarrhines) show foraging ecology consistent with folivory — "similar ecological pressures—the search for young, reddish leaves—may have driven the independent evolution of routine trichromacy in primates on separate continents." — [Melin et al., "Howler monkey foraging ecology suggests convergent evolution of routine trichromacy as an adaptation for folivory," *Ecol Evol* 7:1421–1434 (2017)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5330884/) **[ROBUST primary study; note it is one genus]**
- **The skin-signaling hypothesis (Changizi):** M and L cone sensitivities are closely spaced, and that spacing "maximizes sensitivity for discriminating variations in **blood oxygen saturation**," one of the two blood-related dimensions determining skin reflectance. Hemoglobin's spectral change is "subtle and hard to see, unless there are cones just in the spot where primates have a pair." Critically, **trichromatic primates tend to be bare-faced while dichromatic primates have furry faces** — the correlation the hypothesis rests on. — [Changizi, Zhang & Shimojo, "Bare skin, blood and the evolution of primate colour vision," *Biology Letters* 2:217–221 (2006)](https://www.changizi.com/uploads/8/3/4/4/83445868/colorface.pdf) **[CONTESTED/MODERATE — the correlational bare-skin evidence is suggestive and the optimization modeling is elegant, but this is a smaller literature than the foraging work and the causal direction (did trichromacy enable bare skin, or vice versa?) is not settled]**
- **Clinical corollary that strengthens the skin hypothesis:** human color vision can be harnessed for "visual oximetry in central cyanosis" — we are, in effect, calibrated hemoglobin detectors. — [Changizi & Rio, "Harnessing color vision for visual oximetry in central cyanosis," *Medical Hypotheses* (2010)](https://kevinwrio.com/files/papers/Changizi%20&%20Rio%20(2010)%20Harnessing%20color%20vision%20for%20visual%20oximetry%20in%20central%20cyanosis.pdf) **[MODERATE — Medical Hypotheses is a low-bar journal; use the Biology Letters paper as the primary citation instead]**
- **Also note achromatic contrast matters for short-range fruit foraging** — color is not the whole story even in the foraging account. — [Importance of Achromatic Contrast in Short-Range Fruit Foraging of Primates, PMC2559900](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2559900/) **[ROBUST caveat]**
- **Phylogenetic background facts:** most mammals are dichromats (S + L pigments). All catarrhines (Old World monkeys and apes) plus *Alouatta* have routine trichromacy with M and L λmax near **530 and 560 nm**. Most platyrrhines have sex-linked polymorphic color vision: some females trichromatic, all males dichromatic. S pigment λmax ≈ **430 nm**. — [Sumner & Mollon Ch. 3](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/SumnerMollonFrugivory.pdf) **[ROBUST]**
- **Pluralist current state:** "Trichromacy in primates benefits both frugivory and folivory, suggesting these functions may not be mutually exclusive, and color vision evolution may improve both foraging performance as well as intraspecific communication." — [Surridge, Osorio & Mundy, "Evolution and selection of trichromatic vision in primates," *Trends Ecol Evol* 18:198–205 (2003)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223556020_Evolution_and_selection_of_trichromatic_vision_in_primates_Trends_in_Ecology_and_Evolution_18_198-205) **[ROBUST as a statement of the field's position]**

### Inferences
- **This is the most rhetorically powerful section for the explainer, and the 7×/14× numbers are the money quote.** The red-green channel is not a general-purpose "see more colors" upgrade — it is a special-purpose detector that beats brightness by an order of magnitude at one specific task: *finding the high-nutrient, high-reward item hidden in a cluttered background.* Modern app icons, notification badges, and food/skin-heavy photo feeds are precisely the stimuli that channel was built to find.
- The skin-signaling strand adds the social/emotional half: the same L−M channel reads blushing, pallor, flushing, and cyanosis. A feed of human faces is therefore hitting an evolved emotion-and-health-reading channel. This is a plausible mechanistic story, but it should be presented as *a hypothesis with supporting optimization modeling*, not as established fact.
- Note that both evolutionary accounts point at **ripeness/reward and health/emotion** — i.e., color's job was never information, it was *valuation*. That is exactly the right frame for why grayscale preserves utility and removes pull.

### Gaps
- Could not establish what proportion of the field currently favors each hypothesis (no recent survey or quantitative meta-analysis found). The 2017 Melin paper and the 2003 Surridge review bracket the period; I found no post-2020 review that declares a winner. State the debate as open.

## Q7. Color and emotion/arousal physiology — and what is actually replicable

### Takeaway
**ROBUST:** saturation is the color dimension with the strongest and most consistent effect on
self-reported arousal, and in the best-controlled study it also produced significantly stronger
skin conductance responses, with chromatic stimuli accelerating heart rate where achromatic
(gray) stimuli *decelerated* it. **NOT ROBUST:** virtually the entire "red hue does X" literature,
including the red-attractiveness and red-intelligence effects, which have failed large replication
attempts. The explainer should build on *saturation*, not on *hue*.

### Cited Findings
- **The key study — Wilms & Oberfeld (2018).** Design: 27 chromatic colors from a factorial crossing of hue (blue, green, red) × saturation (low, medium, high) × brightness (dark, medium, bright), **plus 3 brightness-matched achromatic (gray) colors**, specified in CIE L\*a\*b\*, presented on an LED display. **N = 62.** Each color viewed for **30 s**, then rated for current valence and arousal; skin conductance and heart rate measured continuously. Participants rated their *actual induced emotion*, not color-emotion associations. Findings, quoting the abstract: "saturated and bright colors were associated with **higher arousal**"; "The hue also had a significant effect on arousal, which increased from blue and green to red"; "**Saturated and bright colors caused significantly stronger skin conductance responses**"; "**Achromatic colors resulted in a short-term deceleration in the heart rate, while chromatic colors caused an acceleration.**" Also: "Across the 30 colors presented in the experiment, changes in color had a **stronger effect on arousal than on valence**." — [Wilms & Oberfeld, "Color and emotion: effects of hue, saturation, and brightness," *Psychological Research* 82:896–914 (2018)](https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/oberfeld/downloads/Wilms-Oberfeld2018_Article_ColorAndEmotionEffectsOfHueSat.pdf) **[ROBUST — well-powered, colorimetrically controlled, includes an achromatic control, published in a solid journal, and reports both subjective and autonomic measures. This is the single best citation in the whole arousal section.]**
- **Replication of the saturation>hue ordering:** "Saturation had a stronger effect on arousal than the hue, compatible with previous finding by Valdez & Mehrabian (1994)." Valdez & Mehrabian (1994), using the Pleasure-Arousal-Dominance model, "found strong and consistent effects of **higher saturation leading to significantly greater arousal**." — [Wilms & Oberfeld (2018)](https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/oberfeld/downloads/Wilms-Oberfeld2018_Article_ColorAndEmotionEffectsOfHueSat.pdf); Valdez & Mehrabian, *JEP: General* 123:394–409 (1994) **[ROBUST — the saturation→arousal link is the most replicated effect in color-emotion research, confirmed across independent labs and methods]**
- **Background on the physiological measures:** SCR amplitude correlates positively with emotional arousal when viewing emotional pictures (Bradley, Cuthbert, Lang, Roedema), and SCR latency is shorter in high-arousal conditions. **But heart rate's relation to arousal is genuinely disputed:** Wilms & Oberfeld's own literature review notes "results are somewhat disparate. Some authors report an increase of the heart rate with arousal, some a decrease, or no effect at all." — [Wilms & Oberfeld (2018), Introduction](https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/oberfeld/downloads/Wilms-Oberfeld2018_Article_ColorAndEmotionEffectsOfHueSat.pdf) **[Use SCR as the arousal index; treat the HR direction as interpretively ambiguous even though the chromatic/achromatic difference was significant]**
- **Prior hue→SCR work is inconsistent:** skin conductance level higher and SCR stronger for red vs. blue/green (Gerard; Jacobs & Hustmyer; Nourse & Welch; Wilson), "although a study by Mikellides reported no effects." Gerard and Jacobs & Hustmyer found **no effect of hue on heart rate**, while Küller/Mikellides/Janssens reported HR differences by room color. — [Wilms & Oberfeld (2018), Introduction](https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/oberfeld/downloads/Wilms-Oberfeld2018_Article_ColorAndEmotionEffectsOfHueSat.pdf) **[CONTESTED — the hue literature is a mess; say so]**
- **A critical review exists and should be cited as the skeptical counterweight:** "Physiological response to color: A critical review." — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229603356_Physiological_response_to_color_A_critical_review) **[I did not fetch the full text; see Gaps]**
- **⚠️ THE RED-ATTRACTIVENESS EFFECT FAILS TO REPLICATE.** A meta-analysis of Collaborative Replications and Education Project (CREP) replications of Elliot et al.'s (2010) "Red, Rank, and Romance" found **no effect of red on attractiveness ratings** — null for both perceived and sexual attractiveness, "with and without the original data included." Moderator analyses (relationship status) were also null. Other high-powered replications "indicated little to no effect of red on attractiveness," with meta-analysis finding "only a very small effect of men rating women." — [Lehmann, Elliot & Calin-Jageman, "Meta-Analysis of the Effect of Red on Perceived Attractiveness," *Evolutionary Psychology* (2018)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328072536_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Effect_of_Red_on_Perceived_Attractiveness); [Wichman et al., "A Demonstration of the Collaborative Replication and Education Project: Replication Attempts of the Red-Romance Effect," *Collabra: Psychology* 5(1):5 (2019)](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/5/1/5/112984/A-Demonstration-of-the-Collaborative-Replication) **[ROBUST NULL — the brief was right to flag this; it is a textbook replication failure]**
- **The "red impairs intelligence/performance" effect also fails:** "The effect of red on intelligence: meta-analysis and replication." — [OSF preprint](https://files.osf.io/v1/resources/pcy98/providers/osfstorage/6533d56713d27b0c3494d05b?action=download&direct=&version=1) **[Reported as a meta-analysis + replication; I did not fetch the full text, but its existence and framing indicate the effect is contested — flag before quoting a specific number]**

### Inferences
- **The defensible claim for the explainer is about saturation, not hue.** "Saturated color raises arousal, and gray lowers it" is supported by a well-controlled N=62 study with both self-report and autonomic measures, plus independent replication of the saturation ordering. "Red makes you more excited / more attractive / hungrier" is either weakly supported (arousal: yes, modest, in Wilms & Oberfeld) or falsified (attractiveness: no).
- Wilms & Oberfeld's achromatic condition is unusually load-bearing for this project: it is a direct, brightness-matched gray comparison, and it showed a *qualitative* physiological divergence (HR deceleration vs. acceleration). That is close to a laboratory analogue of switching a screen to grayscale.
- Note the study used 30-second full-field color patches, not naturalistic images or interfaces. Generalizing from "a 30 s saturated red field raises SCR" to "a grayscale phone is less arousing" is an inferential leap that the report should make explicitly and cautiously.

### Gaps
- Exact test statistics (F, p, η², b-values) from Wilms & Oberfeld did not survive PDF text extraction. The paper's abstract-level claims are quoted verbatim above and are reliable; **if the explainer needs effect sizes, the paper must be re-opened directly.** I can confirm the direction and significance of every claim above but not the magnitudes.
- Did not verify any claim about color and *appetite* specifically. The brief asked about it; I found no credible primary source in this round. **Treat "color increases appetite" as unverified color-psychology folklore unless a source is found.**
- Did not fetch the "Physiological response to color: A critical review" full text, which would be the best single source for separating robust from shaky physiological claims.

## Q8. Does grayscale viewing measurably reduce arousal, emotional response, and memory?

### Takeaway
Yes on all three, with caveats. Grayscale conversion lowers arousal ratings for natural scenes
and photographs; the effect is content-dependent (strongest for disgust, weak for fear); and
natural color reliably improves recognition memory for scenes by about **5–10%** — but *false*
color does not, which shows the memory benefit comes from matching learned natural color
statistics rather than from "more color = more memorable."

### Cited Findings
- **Arousal is lower in grayscale:** colored photographs "were more arousing than grayscale ones, **independently of the content**." — [Detenber, Simons & Reiss, "The emotional significance of color in television presentations" / and the newspaper-photograph study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233328332_The_impact_of_color_on_emotional_responses_to_newspaper_photographs) **[MODERATE — media-psychology literature, generally small-N; the finding is consistent across several studies but these are not high-powered preregistered designs]**
- "**Grayscale caused significantly lower arousal ratings than original color images**" in a recent study of natural scenes. — [Codispoti et al. / "The influence of color on emotional perception of natural scenes"](https://www.academia.edu/13545045/The_influence_of_color_on_emotional_perception_of_natural_scenes) **[MODERATE — I could not fetch the full text (403); the claim appears in search-result summaries of the paper. FLAG: verify before quoting.]**
- **Content-dependence (important nuance):** arousal ratings were higher for pleasant and unpleasant than neutral images regardless of color, "though unpleasant images were rated slightly less arousing when presented in grayscale." And in a 2026 study: "**achromatic conversion was associated with a reduction in disgust ratings, whereas its effect on fear ratings was comparatively small**" — i.e., grayscale selectively blunts the emotions that depend on color cues (rot, blood, decay) more than those that don't. — [Sato, Muranaka & Motoyoshi, "Color modulates disgust on surfaces, but not fear in scenes," *i-Perception* (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1177/20416695261470309) **[ROBUST for the disgust/fear dissociation; recent and well-targeted]**
- **Valence amplification:** "color had a sizable effect on amplifying the experience of **pleasure and displeasure** in real-life images." — [The effects of color on the enjoyment of real-life images](https://d-nb.info/1308793057/34) **[MODERATE — appears to be a dissertation; verify provenance before citing]**
- **Color and attentional bias to emotional images:** color influences attentional bias to briefly presented emotional images. — [Bringing color to emotion: The influence of color on attentional bias to briefly presented emotional images](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318355403_Bringing_color_to_emotion_The_influence_of_color_on_attentional_bias_to_briefly_presented_emotional_images) **[MODERATE — did not fetch full text]**
- **Memory — the headline number and the crucial control.** "Participants remembered colored natural scenes significantly better than they remembered black and white images, regardless of how long they saw the images." Observers were "**5–10% more accurate** on color-sample/color-test images than all other sample/test combinations," and "are generally more confident in recalling colored than grayscale items, irrespective of whether or not their objective performance differs." **The control that matters: "People did not remember falsely colored natural scenes any better than scenes in black and white"** — so it is natural color, not color per se. Conclusion: "Our memory system is tuned, presumably by evolution and/or during development, to the color structure found in the world." — [Wichmann, Sharpe & Gegenfurtner, "The contributions of color to recognition memory for natural scenes," *JEP: Learning, Memory & Cognition* 28:509–520 (2002)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-contributions-of-color-to-recognition-memory-Wichmann-Sharpe/97338e7c004657422f401056d068d06d34079bba); press summary: [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2002/05/020506073901.htm) **[ROBUST — Gegenfurtner is a leading vision scientist, the design includes the false-color control, and the result has held up]**
- **Color aids *late* but not *early* scene recognition** — so the benefit is at the level of identification/memory consolidation, not initial detection. — [Color aids late but not early stages of rapid natural scene recognition, *J Vision*](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2122013) **[ROBUST and mechanistically informative]**
- **Dichromats confirm the sensory component:** recognition memory for colored vs. black-and-white scenes has been tested in normal and color-deficient observers. — [Recognition Memory for Colored and Black-and-White Scenes in Normal and Color Deficient Observers (Dichromats), PMC4039530](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4039530/) **[Did not fetch; cite only if full text confirms the direction]**
- **Bridge to behavior — the grayscale-phone trials, which the report will likely want.** Cross-over feasibility trial in first-year medical students, **N = 51 completers** (83.6% retention of 61 enrolled), one week per condition: grayscale reduced mean daily screen time by **~28 min/day** (Group A: β = −28.39 min, 95% CI [−51.0, −5.8], p = 0.0143; Group B: β = −28.44 min, 95% CI [−48.0, −8.9], p = 0.0045; combined t = 2.3236, p = 0.0243). The authors' stated mechanism: "color influences eye movement and fixation patterns, suggesting that interface design can shape attentional allocation," characterizing grayscale as working through "**modification of visual salience**" rather than restriction — "an attentional nudge" that reduces "**the visual reward associated with smartphone use**." — [A cross-over feasibility trial of smartphone grayscale mode in medical students, PMC13381430](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13381430/) **[ROBUST design for its size — crossover, significant in both sequence arms independently, which is the right robustness check. But: small N, one population, self-selected, and "feasibility trial" by the authors' own labeling.]**
- **A second, larger grayscale study:** Study 1 (N = 51) found reductions in screen time, problematic smartphone use, and depressive symptoms after two weeks; Study 2 (N = 70) found the grayscale intervention caused larger changes in screen time, problematic smartphone use, and sleep quality than a screen-time-monitoring-only control. Reported reduction: **~1 hour per day**, with problematic-use scores returning to normal for at least six weeks. The authors' proposed mechanism: "smartphone use in grayscale is **less gratifying**, which makes it easier for individuals to regulate their smartphone use." — [Dekker & Baumgartner, "Is life brighter when your phone is not? The efficacy of a grayscale smartphone intervention addressing digital well-being," *Mobile Media & Communication* (2024)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20501579231212062) **[MODERATE-TO-ROBUST — two studies with a control condition in the second; the "1 hour/day" and "6 weeks" figures come from search-result summaries rather than my own reading of the full text. FLAG: verify magnitudes against the paper before putting them in the explainer.]**

### Inferences
- The three literatures line up into a coherent causal chain, which is the explainer's spine: **(1)** color is a first-tier attentional guide (Wolfe & Horowitz) encoded by a channel evolved to find reward (Sumner & Mollon's 14×); **(2)** removing saturation measurably lowers arousal ratings and skin conductance (Wilms & Oberfeld) and lowers emotional response to images (Sato et al.); **(3)** people then use grayscale phones ~28–60 min/day less (PMC13381430; Dekker & Baumgartner), and the trial authors independently converge on "reduced visual salience / less gratifying" as the mechanism.
- The Wichmann false-color control is the most intellectually honest point to include: color's benefit is not generic stimulation, it is a *match to learned natural statistics*. This predicts (and is consistent with) the observation that grayscale's effect may attenuate as users adapt — a point worth noting rather than hiding, and consistent with the Dekker finding that effects persisted at least six weeks but were measured over weeks, not years.
- Caveat the report should carry: the grayscale screen-time trials cannot isolate the chromatic mechanism from novelty, demand characteristics, or the deliberate friction of having chosen an intervention. The biology supplies a *plausible and well-grounded mechanism*; it does not prove that mechanism is what produced the 28 minutes.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch the Codispoti-lineage "influence of color on emotional perception of natural scenes" full text (403 Forbidden on academia.edu). Its arousal finding is reported only via search summary — **verify before quoting.**
- Found no study measuring autonomic arousal (SCR/HR) during *actual grayscale vs. color device use*. That experiment — the one that would directly close the loop between Wilms & Oberfeld and the screen-time trials — appears not to exist. **This is the biggest single gap in the whole causal chain and the report should say so plainly.**
- Did not verify exact effect sizes for the Dekker & Baumgartner studies from the primary text.
- Found no evidence on whether grayscale's effect on arousal *habituates* over months, which is the obvious long-run question.
